## **OPEN Automated violence monitoring system for real-time fistfight detection using deep learningbased temporal action localization** 

**Baolong Qi[1,4] , Baoyuan Wu[2,4] & Bailing Sun[3,4]**[] 

**Fistfight detection in video data is a critical task in video surveillance systems, where identifying physical altercations in real-time can enhance safety and security in public spaces. Earlier techniques primarily emphasized capturing inter-person interactions and combining individual characteristics into group-based representations, often overlooking the critical intra-person dynamics within the human bodypose point framework. However, essential individual features can be extracted by examining human skeletal movements’ progression and temporal patterns. This paper presents a novel multimodal spatio-temporal fistfight detection model (MSTFDet) that integrates RGB images and human skeletal data to identify violent behaviors accurately. The proposed framework leverages both Context-Aware Encoded Transformer (CAET) for modeling interactions between individuals and their environment and Spatial–Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (ST-GCN) for capturing intra-person and inter-person dynamics from skeletal data. The RGB module uses a combination of spatial and temporal transformers to model contextual relationships and individual actions, while the bodyposepoint module processes skeletal data to capture the fine-grained motion of individuals. We conduct evaluations on two public datasets: the Surveillance Camera Fight Dataset (SCFD) and the RWF-2000 dataset, which feature complex real-world scenarios. On the SCFD and RWF-2000 datasets, MSTFDet achieved a Multi-class classification accuracy (MCA) of 92.3% and 95.2% MCA, respectively. These results highlight the effectiveness of the proposed approach in capturing both spatial and temporal features, providing a robust solution for real-time fistfight detection in diverse and challenging environments.**

## **Literature review** 

## **Appearance-based violent detection** 

Appearance-based violence detection methods[27] extract visual features from RGB and optical flow data to model violent behaviors. Early approaches relied on 2D-CNN and 3D-CNN models[28] using optical flow as input to a 2D-CNN to capture motion features. Reference[29] extended 2D convolutions to 3D to improve performance, while Ref.[30] introduced FightNet, which computes optical flow to generate acceleration fields for representing violent interactions. Reference[31] utilized convolutional LSTM networks for violence detection. 

Further advancements include a multi-stream CNN framework[32] , which processes spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal features. This approach efficiently learns environmental and motion patterns relevant to violent behaviors, including fistfights, through modified optical flow and motion energy images. However, appearancebased methods[33][,][34] struggle in complex scenes, with varying lighting and group behavior. Efforts to reduce the computational complexity of 3D-CNN models include lightweight CNN[35] , which processes 16-frame sequences for efficient violence detection. Reference[36] proposed a DenseNet-based 3D-CNN to reduce parameters through feature reuse. Reference[37] introduced a method that uses 3D convolutions to model spatial–temporal relationships in violent interactions, demonstrating strong efficiency and real-world applicability. In contrast, unsupervised methods have also gained attention. Reference[38] proposed Double-AE, a double-stream AutoEncoder that filters background noise and extracts spatial–temporal features for violence detection. To address the scarcity of labeled violence data, Ref.[39] introduced an unsupervised Spatio Temporal Action Translation (STAT) network for detecting violent behaviors. 

More recently, Efficient-X3D[40] applied transfer learning with pre-trained video models for anomaly detection in crowded scenes, improving computational efficiency and reducing overfitting. PiercingEye[41] proposed a dual-space learning framework combining Euclidean and hyperbolic feature spaces, achieving finegrained discrimination of visually similar events using a hyperbolic vision-language guidance strategy. LightWVAD[42] , a lightweight weakly supervised framework, further reduced model complexity and label noise using adaptive instance selection and multi-level temporal correlation attention. Event-based data has also emerged as a promising direction; UCF-Crime-DVS[43] introduced the first event-driven anomaly detection dataset based on Dynamic Vision Sensors (DVS), enabling high-temporal-resolution representations for violence detection. 

## **BodyPose-based violent detection** 

ST-LSTM[44] employs recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to model the temporal dynamics inherent in skeletal data. Specifically, the structural information of the human skeleton is input into a LSTM network, which captures the temporal evolution of joint positions across consecutive frames. This approach leverages the sequential nature of skeletal movements, enabling the model to learn the temporal dependencies between individual joint movements over time. The LSTM’s ability to retain long-term dependencies in the data makes it particularly suitable for modeling complex, continuous actions, such as those involved in fistfights, where the coordination and timing of movements are crucial for accurate recognition. In contrast, convolutional neural network (CNN)based approaches encode the temporal dynamics of skeletal data by converting skeleton joints into matrix representations, treating the sequences as image-like structures. These matrices, which encapsulate both spatial (joint locations) and temporal (movement across frames) information, are processed by CNNs to recognize actions in a manner akin to image classification tasks. This technique allows CNNs to exploit their ability to capture spatial hierarchies in data while learning temporal patterns. For instance, Hierarchical Co-occurrence Networks (HCN)[45] utilize an end-to-end learning framework where CNNs are employed to automatically extract hierarchical co-occurrence features from skeleton sequences. These features capture both the spatial configuration of joints and their temporal transitions, enabling robust action recognition in dynamic settings. 

ST-GCN[46] represents a significant advancement in the field by extending the concept of neural networks to graph structures. ST-GCN is designed to process skeletal data by treating human skeleton joints as nodes in a topological graph, with the edges representing the relationships between joints. This spatiotemporal graph model allows for a more natural and flexible representation of human motion, as it directly captures the spatial and temporal dependencies between joints. The GCN used in ST-GCN acts as the backbone for behavior recognition, applying convolutions over the graph structure to learn both the spatial configuration of joints and their temporal evolution. However, while ST-GCN is effective in modeling skeleton-based actions, it faces challenges when integrating with other modalities, such as RGB data and optical flow, as its complexity increases linearly with the number of individuals in the scene. This scalability issue can limit its applicability in real-time or large-scale action detection tasks. 

Recent approaches have sought to address these limitations by treating skeleton sequences as 3D point clouds, which allows for the learning of contextual relationships between individuals from skeletal points. This is particularly relevant for the detection of violent behaviors, such as fistfights, where multiple individuals are often involved in complex, highly dynamic interactions[1][,][47] . In this context, skeleton-based methods offer a structured and interpretable representation of human actions, but they often struggle to capture the fine-grained local motion patterns that are critical for recognizing violent interactions in real-world scenarios.

## **RGB modality** 

In the initial step, T-frame images are used as input to extract individual features through the Image Feature Extractor. Subsequently, the Context-Aware Encoded Transformer (CAET) integrates contextual information to refine and enhance these individual features. Given the dynamic nature and interactions inherent in both individual fistfight actions and group activities, the Spatial Transformer (STrans) and Temporal Transformer (TTrans) is employed to explore the spatio-temporal relationships within the enhanced individual features. Finally, the outputs of the STrans and TTrans are concatenated, followed by the application of a pooling layer to derive the group-level features from the Image module. Unlike STrans, which models spatial relations across different individuals, S-GCN focuses exclusively on intra-person spatial configurations, leveraging skeletal topologies to capture joint-level anatomical constraints. 

Although our system processes a fixed number of frames _T_ centered around the key action frame, we mitigate the limitations associated with this temporal window size through several architectural design choices. First, the use of the Temporal Transformer (TTrans) enables the model to capture long-range dependencies across frames within the _T_ -length window using self-attention, without relying on sequential operations that often dilute memory of earlier frames. Furthermore, the sliding-window processing strategy ensures that overlapping windows cover the entire video, allowing delayed or prolonged violent actions to be captured in adjacent sequences. Thus, while the temporal length _T_ is fixed, our system effectively maintains robustness to long-range or delayed behaviors through attention-based modeling and temporal window redundancy. 

## _RGB feature extractor_ 

The RGB module processes the input video by receiving _T_ -frame sequences, where each sequence is centered around the key frame that contains the labeled action. This method ensures that both the preceding and following temporal contexts are captured effectively, aiding in the recognition of dynamic actions in violent scenarios. To extract meaningful spatial–temporal features from these input frames, we employ a robust backbone network, such as EfficientNet or ResNet, which is specifically designed for high-performance feature extraction in vision tasks. This backbone processes the _T_ -frame sequences to generate feature maps, denoted as _X ∈_ R _[T][ ×][D][×][H][×][W]_ , where _T_ represents the temporal dimension, _D_ is the depth of the feature map, and _H_ and _W_ correspond to the spatial height and width of each frame, respectively. To isolate the actions of individual persons within the scene, we apply Region of Interest (RoI) alignment using the bounding boxes of _N_ individuals. RoIAlign, as described in Ref.[51] , is particularly advantageous because it accurately aligns the features of each individual, maintaining the spatial integrity of their movements and positions, which is crucial for fine-grained action detection such as fistfights. The RoIAlign operation crops and resizes the feature maps to focus on the regions within the bounding boxes, ensuring that each individual is represented by distinct spatial features. 

After RoIAlign, the refined individual feature maps are passed through a fully connected (FC) layer, transforming them into _d_ -dimensional vectors. These vectors represent the features of each individual in the scene across the _T_ frames, and are expressed as _XI ∈_ R _[T][ ×][N][×][d]_ , where _N_ represents the number of individuals, and _d_ is the feature dimension of each individual. The FC layer serves to condense the high-dimensional feature maps into a more compact and discriminative representation that highlights the key attributes of each person’s actions, such as motion patterns, posture, and contextual interactions with others. By converting the extracted feature maps into _d_ -dimensional representations, this process enables the system to effectively capture critical spatial and temporal details associated with violent interactions, such as the speed, direction, and intensity of movements, which are essential for accurately identifying fistfights. The resulting _XI_ matrix contains rich spatial-temporal information, providing a robust foundation for the subsequent stages of action detection, where further refinement and classification of violent behaviors, including punching, kicking, and grappling, can occur within the broader spatial context of the scene.

## _Temporal transformer_ 

Temporal dynamics play a crucial role in understanding the progression of individual actions, particularly in scenarios involving group activities like fistfights, where interactions unfold over time. To effectively capture these dynamics, we introduce the Temporal Transformer (TTrans), which is designed to encode the temporal evolution of individual features. The TTrans is integral to modeling the sequence of actions over consecutive frames, ensuring that the temporal relationships between individuals are preserved and leveraged for improved action recognition as shown in Fig. 3. Previous approaches, such as the Inflated 3D ConvNet (I3D) models, attempted to capture temporal information by expanding 2D convolutional networks into the temporal domain to process image sequences directly[52] . However, these models often neglected the fine-grained temporal dynamics of individual actions, instead focusing on aggregated or pooled representations across the entire sequence. In contrast, the TTrans leverages an attention-based mechanism that is well-suited for capturing both shortterm and long-term dependencies across frames. The self-attention mechanism within the TTrans allows it to adapt to the varying importance of different time steps, ensuring that the features of individuals are refined based on their temporal context. This attention mechanism not only identifies relevant temporal patterns but also mitigates the risk of losing important individual-level temporal information, which is a common drawback of traditional pooling techniques. SCFD focuses on fine-grained classification of specific fistfight-related actions, while RWF-2000 was constructed for broader violence detection, using coarser categories like “violent” and “non-violent.” For experimental consistency, we extracted the most comparable and relevant fistfight-related actions from both datasets. ## **Evaluation metrics** 

To evaluate the performance of our MSTFDet model, we utilize a set of standard classification metrics, including Precision, Recall, and F1-Score. These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of the model’s ability to accurately detect and classify fistfight-related actions while minimizing false positives and negatives. Precision measures the proportion of true positives (correctly detected actions) among all positive predictions made by the model, providing insight into the accuracy of the detections. Recall, on the other hand, assesses the model’s ability to identify all relevant instances of fistfight actions within the dataset, capturing how well the model can detect true positives without missing critical instances. F1-Score offers a harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a balanced metric that accounts for both false positives and false negatives. 

In addition to these metrics, we also report Multi-class Classification Accuracy (MCA), which measures the overall accuracy of the model across all classes, including both violent and non-violent behaviors. This metric is particularly useful for understanding the model’s general performance in detecting different types of actions.

## **Discussion** 

The presented MSTFDet framework demonstrates a advancement in the field of violence detection, particularly focused on identifying complex, dynamic interactions such as fistfights from surveillance video sequences. The extensive experiments conducted on the Surveillance Camera Fight Dataset (SCFD) and RWF-2000 dataset underscore the efficacy and robustness of our multimodal approach, which strategically integrates visual (RGB) and structural (skeletal) information through sophisticated spatio-temporal modeling techniques. 

A key observation derived from our results is the effectiveness of multimodal data fusion. By leveraging complementary strengths of both RGB-based and bodypose-based features, MSTFDet significantly enhances its ability to discern subtle action cues, thus improving overall detection accuracy. The high performance across diverse fistfight actions such as punching, slapping, and grappling validates the rationale of combining contextual RGB features with structured skeletal data. Skeletal data, in particular, proved vital in capturing finegrained intra-person dynamics that RGB frames alone could overlook due to occlusions or visual ambiguities common in surveillance environments. 

Our ablation study provided deeper insights into the individual contributions of each module. Within the RGB module, combining the CAET with both Spatial and Temporal Transformers (STrans and TTrans) demonstrated the highest performance gains. Specifically, CAET effectively encodes contextual interactions between individuals and their environment, significantly reducing false positives that typically arise from misinterpretations of aggressive postures in non-violent contexts. The STrans component efficiently captured critical spatial relationships, accurately discerning interactions among multiple individuals. At the same time, TTrans effectively encoded temporal dynamics, essential for interpreting the progression of violent actions over consecutive frames. Results indicated that omitting any of these components compromised performance, reinforcing the importance of comprehensive spatio-temporal modeling. 

Similarly, the bodypose module, based on ST-GCN, exhibited robust performance, particularly highlighting the importance of combined spatial and temporal modeling over isolated spatial or temporal approaches. The optimal configuration was identified at a 7-layered ST-GCN, balancing computational complexity with generalization capabilities. Deeper architectures offered marginal performance improvements but posed risks of overfitting and higher computational demands, thus emphasizing the necessity for balanced architectural depth. 

Analysis of the confusion matrices further elucidated specific challenges in distinguishing visually similar actions, particularly when interactions involved close physical contacts such as grappling and choking. Although MSTFDet showed strong overall performance, minor confusions between closely related violent actions revealed inherent difficulties due to occlusions and indistinct spatial boundaries in surveillance scenarios. Future models could potentially address these challenges through more advanced attention mechanisms that dynamically weight skeletal joints and spatial regions based on their visibility and discriminative significance. 

Another important design consideration is the use of a fixed temporal window _T_ , which could appear to restrict the model’s ability to detect actions with long latency or gradual build-up. However, this limitation is alleviated by the inclusion of TTrans, which enable global attention over all _T_ frames, capturing both short- and long-term dependencies. ## **Failure cases and group size scalability** 

Although MSTFDet achieves high classification accuracy in controlled group interactions, certain limitations emerge when analyzing scenes involving more than two individuals. Specifically, in highly crowded settings (e.g., > 3 individuals engaged in overlapping interactions), the accuracy of spatial relation modeling degrades due to inter-person occlusions and increased visual clutter. This results in higher confusion rates between visually similar actions such as grappling and pushing or choking and elbow strikes, especially when multiple persons perform overlapping or concurrent gestures. 

To investigate scalability across varying group sizes, we conducted a stratified performance evaluation based on the number of individuals present in each scene. As summarized in Table 9, MSTFDet maintains strong accuracy for group sizes up to three individuals (MCA > 90%), but performance declines modestly in scenes with four or more individuals, where MCA drops to 86.4% due to increased inter-personal ambiguity and tracking inconsistencies. This reflects the intrinsic challenge of modeling high-density physical interactions using global attention and graph-based reasoning under occlusion and clutter. 

While the STrans module dynamically constructs spatial interaction graphs per frame, extremely dense configurations may still obscure key relationships. Future iterations may benefit from integrating group-aware attention strategies or hierarchical modeling of sub-groups to better represent multi-party dynamics in complex scenes. 

## **Limitations** 

While both the SCFD and RWF-2000 datasets offer rich annotations and a range of violent behaviors in varied public settings, they share a common constraint: the majority of the surveillance footage is captured from frontfacing or slightly oblique camera perspectives. These views do not adequately represent scenarios involving extreme viewing angles such as top-down surveillance (common in ceiling-mounted CCTV) or fisheye lenses (used in wide-angle monitoring). Consequently, the generalization of the MSTFDet model to such perspectives remains unverified. These camera angles can introduce significant geometric distortions, occlusions, or altered spatial relationships among individuals, which may degrade the model’s performance. Future work should consider incorporating datasets featuring a broader spectrum of camera viewpoints including top-down, fisheye, or mobile surveillance to systematically evaluate and enhance the model’s robustness across diverse surveillance environments. Furthermore, adaptation techniques such as domain generalization, synthetic data augmentation, or viewpoint-invariant feature learning could be explored to mitigate the impact of perspective variations on model performance. 

Another limitation pertains to dataset specificity. Although SCFD and RWF-2000 datasets represent diverse violent behaviors across various environmental contexts, real-world surveillance scenarios can exhibit even greater complexities, including extreme lighting variations, partial occlusions, and rapidly evolving multiperson interactions. Addressing these conditions necessitates expanded datasets encompassing broader action categories and environmental conditions, thus enabling further generalization and robustness enhancements. Moreover, while MSTFDet demonstrates strong performance in two- and three-person interactions, our analysis reveals a measurable drop in accuracy in highly crowded scenes involving four or more individuals. This performance dip is attributed to increased occlusion, motion overlap, and graph connectivity challenges, and motivates future work on scalable representations for group dynamics. 

Although our system processes a fixed number of frames _T_ centered around the key action frame, we mitigate the limitations associated with this temporal window size through several architectural design choices. First, the use of the Temporal Transformer (TTrans) enables the model to capture long-range dependencies across frames within the _T_ -length window using self-attention, without relying on sequential operations that often dilute memory of earlier frames. Second, the fixed temporal context (e.g., 10 frames before and after the anchor) is chosen based on empirical analysis to cover the typical temporal extent of violent actions in surveillance footage, such as punching or grappling. Third, the sliding-window processing strategy ensures that overlapping windows cover the entire video, allowing delayed or prolonged violent actions to be captured in adjacent sequences. Thus, while the temporal length _T_ is fixed, our system effectively maintains robustness to long-range or delayed behaviors through attention-based modeling and temporal window redundancy.

## **Conclusion** 

In this paper, we introduced a novel multimodal spatio-temporal fistfight detection model (MSTFDet), which leverages both RGB image data and body pose-point data to address the complexities inherent in detecting violent behaviors such as fistfights within surveillance footage. The model’s dual-modality architecture, comprising an RGB module and a bodypose-point module, demonstrated its efficacy in capturing both interperson and intra-person dynamics. Through the integration of CAET, STrans, and TTrans for the RGB module, coupled with ST-GCN for pose estimation, our MSTFDet model was able to effectively model both spatial and temporal relationships, enhancing detection accuracy. Experimental results on two benchmark datasets, SCFD and RWF-2000, showcased the robustness of the proposed model in accurately classifying violent and non-violent interactions across diverse environmental conditions. Our model outperformed several state-ofthe-art approaches, achieving a Multi-class Classification Accuracy (MCA) of 92.3% on the SCFD dataset and 95.2% on the RWF-2000 dataset. The results highlight the importance of leveraging both visual and skeletal data to capture nuanced physical interactions, which are critical for real-time violence detection in complex scenarios. The ablation studies further demonstrated that the integration of spatial and temporal components significantly improves the performance of the model, with the optimal configuration being the combination of all components for both modalities. 

Future research directions could focus on optimizing the model’s scalability for real-time applications in large-scale surveillance systems and addressing challenges related to occlusions, varying illumination conditions, and group behavior dynamics. Additionally, exploring more efficient architectures to reduce computational complexity without compromising accuracy would be valuable for deploying such models in resource-constrained environments.
