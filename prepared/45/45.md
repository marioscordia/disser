## **1. Introduction** 

Both the business world and academia find video surveillance to be an important and exciting research topic [1,2]. Due to the growing safety concerns [3] in many areas, including train stations, airports, military sites, and retail centers, video surveillance is receiving a lot of interest in the research community. Since it’s hard to make detection techniques without knowing about the target activity and the scene, it’s important to find out if suspicious things are happening there [3]. In order to locate and identify anomalies in a complex environment, video surveillance is mostly focused on crowded situations. An anomaly is an unusual event or an irregular occurrence. The effort of modeling and processing the anomalous scene outcomes might be difficult and might even seem impossible [4,5]. The identification of anomalies in video surveillance is a key activity that may be challenging since anomalies are defined as behaviors that deviate from the established patterns. An anomalous activity may be a typical activity in certain settings, such as a shooting at a gun club. In shooting clubs, shooting is normal despite the fact that it is often an abnormal behavior. As an alternative, certain behaviors may not necessarily be abnormal but still be anomalous under 

## certain settings [6,7]. 

Killing, looting, molestation, and intense assaults are only a few examples of the abnormal behaviors that are often noted. Killing is the intentional taking of another person’s life. Looting is the act of taking someone else’s possessions by employing severe physical force and violence. Sexual exploitation of individuals (men, women, and children) against their will is molestation. This unlawful action is horrible and has serious repercussions. Intensive assaults are unlawful conflicts between people that are carried out to get something or cause damage to others [8]. A system that grabs notice is anomaly detection and prevention utilizing deep learning. To protect public safety, several departments of law enforcement throughout the world are experimenting with deep learning (DL) systems. A law enforcement department may learn from the predicted anomaly operations and large volume of data processing that reveal anomalous patterns. Anomaly activities can go unnoticed because of external influences coming from all sectors of society. Because of this, an intelligent security system may identify anomalous activity on its own and aid in excluding manipulative activity by alerting law enforcement agencies and avoiding people. In order to extract crucial information from recorded videos, computer vision automates the identification of anomalous behaviors by alerting a system that enforces the law when anomalous activities are observed [10,11]. From the standpoint of DL, the classification of anomalous activities is divided into supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised learning models. One deep learning approach trains the model on both typical and atypical behaviors [16]. On the other hand, in a multi-model learning context, deep learning models are trained using both normal and anomalous activity [17]. Several research studies used supervised deep learning to find anomalous behaviors in videos [18–25]. For anomaly detection, a variety of DL models are used, including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long-Short Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), Simple Recurrent Unit (SRUs), and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [26–30].

An anomaly detection system’s purpose is to predict and prevent anomalous (criminal) actions. Although traditional, non-deep learning techniques are helpful, yet they act on their own. As a result, a machine capable of integrating the key components of traditional methodologies would be tremendously beneficial. Three models are used which are linear regression, additive regression, and decision stump. The linear regression on randomly selected samples in testing was able to manage unpredictability, demonstrating improved detection across models and demonstrating the 

effectiveness of deep learning in recognizing violent trends and criminal tendencies.

The results indicate that fortnight predictions improve extraordinarily as compare to the monthly predictions. Anomaly activities are identified and observed in Ref. [33] utilizing anomaly data for the last 15 years from the Vancouver city. The KNN and decision tree detected anomaly activities. Another study [34] predicted anomaly statistics in Philadelphia to determine anomaly trends. ML algorithms (KNN, logistic regression, Ordinal regression, and decision trees) were used to train for getting anomaly predictions with large datasets. The models were able to determine the trend of anomaly activity with 69% accuracy. Models using data science were applied to detect the anomalies in the Chicago criminal dataset. Various ML models (SVM, logistic regression, decision trees, KNN, random forest, and Bayesian models) were tested and nominated as precise models for training. The best accuracy (78.7%) was achieved with the KNN. This study suggested that law enforcement agencies use deep learning models to predict, find, and solve unusual activities more quickly. A GUI-based deep learning model for predicting the anomalies is proposed in Ref. [35]. The results of supervised models are compared to predicted anomalies. Over a period of 12 years, the deep learning architecture was trained on criminal activity in San Francisco, California where 99.2% accuracy was attained by using oversampling and undersampling with random forest. Spatiotemporal networks (STNs) are gaining popularity to learn spatial and temporal features [44] where RNNs and CNNs jointly extract spatiotemporal features for anomaly detection. Another model where a convolutional layer filters the output of CNNs before feeding an LSTM is called the ConvLSTM [45]. An alternative to the fully connected layer in LSTM, a convolutional layer intensely reduces the number of parameters. The GRU further reduce the parameters if replaced with LSTM in ConvLSTM. ## **3. Proposed video anomaly detection system** 

Fig. 1 shows the diagram of the proposed video anomaly detection system in videos. To extract features from DNNs, residual networks (ResNets) are efficient neural models [46]. In order to extract spatial information from the input video frames, ResNet must first be deployed. The next step is to extract the temporal dependencies in video frames using SRU as an RNN model. As inputs for ResNet, the videos are split up into sequences of m frames, and the SRU is then fed the outputs of the ResNet. To find the anomaly in video frames, the spatiotemporal attributes are processed through maxpooling and fully-connected layers. The ResNet-18, ResNet-34, and ResNet-50 have been used as CNN models in the proposed system for anomaly detection to extract high-level data from the video frames. 

## _3.1. Video pre-processing_ 

The proposed CNN + SRU system for anomaly detection in videos is shown in Fig. 1, where input video is first preprocessed and segmented into fixed frames with a frame rate of 30 frames per second. As a result, there are 1800 total frames in a 60-s video. After selecting the frames, the difference between each frame and its neighboring frame is computed in order to take all the input frames’ spatial motions into account. We chose three categories from the UCF-Crime dataset. Each video’ s specific period of anomalous activity was divided into two halves and labeled as an anomaly; the remaining video was given the label normal. The videos are then separated into segments of equal duration. Thus, from m frames, k frames are chosen. Only anomalous activities are thus highlighted. Additionally, the regular activities are chosen from the identical videos that include the abnormal activities. Other than actions, every setting is the same as it was in the UCF-Crime dataset. Such configurations improve the system’s ability to identify anomalous activity. Full training videos incur significant computational expense. As a result, we have taken into consideration a training framework over a predetermined number of frames, namely, a targeted bag that includes the key information required to interpret the actions in 

the videos, followed by block formulation and selection. A targeted bag is a group of frames made up of actions in a full-length recorded video. The extraction of this targeted bag is shown in Fig. 2, where just a small proportion of the complete recorded video is identified as suspicious or criminal actions. With Z-frames from W-frames, they are thus seen as a targeted bag. By using this method on every video feed in the database, the amount of training data was greatly cut down by getting rid of duplicate information. 

## _3.2. ResNet CNN models_ 

On several common datasets, including ImageNet, ResNet has shown outstanding performance. There are several variations of ResNets, including ResNet-18, ResNet-34, ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and ResNet152. However, CNN + SRU have been implemented on ResNet-18, ResNet-34, and ResNet-50 because of its superior design and performance for our tasks. The proposed approach employs Transfer Learning [47,48] to prevent problems with labeling anomalies, and CNN + SRU is thus pre-trained using the ImageNet dataset, which consists of 1000 sets of images. The model parameters are loaded and updated by running ResNet on ImageNet, making it ready to run on the desired datasets. The ResNet can process (240 × 240 × 3) dimension data due to the input frame size of (240 × 240). A 4-D tensor (n × 1 × 1 × 2048) output is created from the Deep Residual Features (DRF) while passing through the convolutional and pooling layers, which is then reshaped before being fed to the SRUs. Fig. 3 shows the ResNet-18, ResNet-34, and ResNet-50 structures, and Table 2 contains the architecture of three CNN models. The outputs of ResNet are transformed into (n × 3 × 3 × 128) and given to the SRU layers. A fully connected dense layer is not used since ResNet does not use it for categorization. Due to improved parallelization and gradient propagation, the parts work together to create a basic yet powerful design that is also simple to scale. 

## _3.3. SRU layer_ 

Due to the inherent difficulties of parallelizing their state calculations, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) scale poorly. The Simple Recurrent Unit (SRU) [49] in this study is a lightweight recurrent unit that establishes a balance between model capacity and scalability. For the purpose of facilitating the training of deep models, SRU is built to provide expressive recurrence, allow highly parallelized implementation, and include rigorous initialization. We have applied to the task of anomaly detection in video frames to show the efficiency of SRU when combined with CNNs. On the classification datasets, we assume that SRU outperforms LSTM and convolutional models and is faster than optimized LSTM. Incorporating SRU into the design also allows us to translate data more efficiently than the Transformer model. This section introduces and describes the SRU design. ## **4. Experiments** 

## _4.1. Dataset and evaluation measures_ 

In this paper, the proposed model is implemented on the UCF-Crime dataset [31] which includes abnormal, illegal, and violent behavior recorded by surveillance video cameras located in public places such as stores and streets. The UCF-Crime dataset is prepared from everyday 

actual events, which is the key reason for selecting this dataset. Many studies have relied on handicraft datasets or specific datasets with similar backgrounds and environments (for example, fighting and movie datasets), which are unrelated to our daily lives. The UCFCrime dataset includes lengthy surveillance video camera feeds covering 13 different classes of anomaly events such as abuse, arrest, arson, assault, road accidents, burglaries, explosions, fighting, robbery, shooting, stealing, shoplifting, and vandalism in addition to the normal events class. For comparison with other related studies, the training and testing data is arranged as 75% and 25% in experiments. The example samples of the UCF-Crime dataset are given in Fig. 5. Two variants of the UCF-Crime dataset, including Ucfcrimes and Binary, are used in the experiments, where Ucfcrimes contains 14 classes whereas Binary has 13 abnormal events. The quantity of videos for each class from the UCFCrime and Binary datasets is given in Table 3. The model is evaluated for accuracy, precision, F1, and AUC, respectively. 

## _4.2. Model setting and selection_ 

In experiments, the proposed model is applied by using ResNet-18, ResNet-34, and ResNet-50, which are available in the Keras library. To tune the model, several hyperparameters are used to attain the best performance. Table 4 shows the results of experiments with different types of weight initialization and optimizers. As a result, to initialize the model weights, glorot-uniform (Xavier) is utilized, whereas to optimize the model, the RMSprop optimizer is imposed. The learning rate and the number of epochs are fixed at 0.0001 and 100, respectively. However, an early stop is applied when the loss converges. The video sequence length is fixed at 20 frames. Since then, a focused bag has been used for video frames. Table 5 provides a comparison of total video frames and frames in the focus bag. In experiments, different kinds of evaluation are used. During the first step, CNN-SRU is tested with several CNN models such as InceptionV3, VGG19, ResNet-18, ResNet-34, and ResNet-50 available in the Keras library. Table 6 shows a comparison in terms of accuracy. Keras and Tensor flow platforms are used for simulations.

## **7. Conclusions** 

This study proposes a novel deep learning framework by integrating ResNet-50/34/18 and SRU models for detecting anomaly activities in the UCF-Crime and binary datasets. A few anomalies take place in videos where people cannot be seen, such as car accidents. Besides, other anomaly events happen for a few seconds in a short video (10 s), but most parts of such videos show a normal event. Regardless of stated limitations, the CNN + SRU models outscored other models on the UCFCrime dataset with 90.20% AUC and 90.19% accuracy (average of three models that are ResNet18 + SRU, ResNet34 + SRU, and ResNet50 + SRU). In addition to the 14 classes of the UCF-Crime dataset, dividing the dataset into two major classes (Ucfcrimes and Binary) shows improved results in terms of accuracy and AUC. The focused video frames extracted from the original videos of anomaly events have significantly improved the detection accuracy. Among other CNN models implemented for anomaly events, ResNet-50 combined with SRU provides improved results. With excellent feature extraction, ResNet50 and SRU significantly improved the performance measures.
