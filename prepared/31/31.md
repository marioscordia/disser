## **ABSTRACT** 

The rapid growth of video data has resulted in an increasing need for surveillance and violence detection systems. Although such events occur less frequently than normal activities, developing automated video surveillance systems for violence detection has become essential to minimize labor and time waste. Detecting violent activity in videos is a challenging task due to the variability and diversity of violent behavior, which can involve a wide range of actions, motions, and interactions between people and objects. Currently, researchers employ deep learning models to detect violent behaviors. In fact, a large number of deep learning approaches are based on extracting spatio-temporal information from a video by exploiting a 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Despite ~~their success, these techniques require a lot more parameters than 2D CNNs and have high computational complexity. Therefore,~~ we focus on exploiting a 2D CNN to encode spatio-temporal information. Actually, statistical features of the optical flow changes are used to give this ability to a 2D CNN. These features are designed to make attention to regions of a video clip with much more motion. Accordingly, the optical flow of an input video is calculated. To determine meaningful changes in the optical flow, the optical flow magnitude of a current frame is compared with its predecessor. After that, statistical features of these changes are extracted to summarize a video clip to a 2D template, which feeds a 2D CNN. Experimental results on four benchmark datasets observe that the suggested strategy outperforms baseline ones. In particular, we make a better estimation of the spatio-temporal features in a video by shortening a video clip into a 2D template. 

## **1 | Introduction** 

One of the difficult issues in video surveillance systems is violence detection. Traditionally, this task has been carried out by an operator who manually tracks videos. However, automated violence detection systems have become more common in recent years. Despite the potential usefulness of such systems, violence detection had been less studied until Bermejo Nievas et al. [1] created two datasets: Hockey Fight and Action Movies. Unfortunately, these two datasets are not proper for analyzing crowded 

scenes. Therefore, Hassner et al. [2] created a new dataset called Violent Flows. 

Nowadays, the use of violence detection systems is increasing in smart cities to monitor citizens. Cameras installed in public places, such as metros, prisons, schools, and streets, help security guards to control dangerous and violent behaviors. As a result, there is a growing demand for automatic violence detection systems. Furthermore, there is an increasing need to classify uploaded videos on the internet to mitigate the harmful effects

This work focuses on identifying violent scenes in video clips captured by surveillance cameras. A video is composed of a series of slightly different images shown sequentially to give the impression of continuous motion. To detect action in a video, spatial and temporal features are specifically exploited. Space–Time Interest Points (STIP) [3] and Motion SIFT [4] were two traditional methods for this purpose. Nowadays, deep neural networks are popularly used to extract spatio-temporal features. For example, Tran et al. [5] introduced a 3D CNN to do this task by expanding 2D kernels to 3D ones. Their proposed network was implemented on the UCF101 dataset for action recognition. To improve the performance of 3D CNNs, Simonyan et al. [6] exploited two-stream CNNs that used frames and their optical flows as inputs. However, both streams were designed according to a 3D CNN architecture, and action recognition was accomplished by combining the results of two CNNs. Although 3D CNNs and two-stream architectures have shown improved performance, their high computational requirements make them challenging to use. In our work, we focus on using a lightweight 2D CNN as an alternative. To achieve this, we generate a 2D template from each video by combining statistical features of the optical flow changes to encode spatial and temporal features. 

Some works exploited spatial and temporal attention modules to improve the performance of deep neural networks. For example, Liang et al. [7] presented a Temporal Shift Module (TSM) and a channel attention module to strengthen their model. Another work was proposed by Rendón-Segador et al. [8]. They encoded spatio-temporal features with an attention module. They considered the optical flow of frames as inputs of a dense network to extract features, which were then processed further by a bidirectional ConvLSTM. Attention modules typically demanded significant memory usage and computational complexity because inferring attention was a non-trivial task [9]. Unlike methods that rely on attention modules, our approach utilizes statistical features of optical flow changes to summarize spatio-temporal information into a 2D matrix, significantly maintaining high accuracy and simplicity in violence detection. Our method is simple and easy to implement, as it does not require complex attention mechanisms or additional training procedures. It only utilizes statistical features of optical flow changes, which are easy to compute and interpret. 

This article focuses on exploiting a 2D CNN to encode spatio-temporal features. Our violence detection system includes two main steps, which are described as follows: 

- In a violent scene, there are typically more movements compared to a non-violent scene. To identify significant changes in pixels, the optical flow of each frame is computed and compared with its predecessor. 

- The statistical features of changes in the optical flow are exploited to enable a 2D CNN for encoding spatio-temporal information. These features aim to identify meaningful regions and changes in frames. The statistical features are 

averaged across all frames to form a 2D template, which is then used as the input for a 2D CNN. 

Our intuition of the proposed method is summarized as follows: 

- a. We exploit the observation that violent scenes tend to have more movements than non-violent scenes. To capture this, we obtain the optical flow of each frame and compare it with its predecessor to determine meaningful changes in pixels. By doing this, we can identify regions of the scene where the action is taking place and better understand the dynamics of the scene. Then, we use statistical features of the changes in the optical flow to enable a 2D CNN to encode spatio-temporal information. Instead of using raw pixel values, we identify meaningful changes in the scene and the statistical features of these changes to create a 2D template that is then sent into the CNN. This approach allows us to focus on the most important features of the scene and extract the relevant information more efficiently. 

- b. 3D CNNs have been widely used to encode spatio-temporal features in video data. However, they have many more parameters than 2D CNNs, which increase the computational complexity of the model. This can make it challenging to deploy 3D CNNs on devices with limited computing resources, such as mobile devices. To tackle this problem, we propose to exploit a simple 2D CNN to encode spatio-temporal features. Instead of using a 3D CNN, we summarize a video clip into a 2D template and use it as input to a 2D CNN. This approach significantly reduces the number of parameters in the model, making it more practical for real-world applications. To create the 2D template, we utilize the statistical features of the optical flow, as mentioned earlier. These statistical features capture the spatio-temporal dynamics of the scene and enable us to identify meaningful regions and changes in the frames. We then summarize these features across all frames into a 2D matrix, which represents the overall statistical features of the scene. This 2D matrix serves as the input to the 2D CNN. By using a 2D CNN instead of a 3D CNN, we can achieve better performance in violence detection while significantly reducing the model’s complexity and parameters. 

The remainder of this essay is structured as follows. Some related works are explained in Section 2. The suggested strategy is provided in Section 3. In Section 4, the experimental results are presented and discussed. In Section 5, we finally conclude. 

## **2 | Related Works** 

Methods for violence detection can be broadly classified into two categories: traditional methods and those based on deep learning. Traditional approaches rely on extracting handcrafted features, which are manually obtained. In contrast, deep learning techniques take advantage of deep neural network topologies, most frequently 3D CNNs and LSTMs.

## **2.1 | Traditional Approaches** 

Traditional approaches for violence detection often involve extracting handcrafted features from either the audio or visual features. For instance, audio-based methods may extract features such as the amplitude or frequency of certain sounds, while visual-based methods may extract features such as color, texture, or motion. However, these approaches have limitations, as they rely on manual feature extraction and may not capture all the relevant information for violence detection. 

As a result, researchers have explored the combination of both audio and visual features through audio-visual analysis. This approach can provide a more comprehensive understanding of violent events by capturing not only the visual aspects but also the accompanying audio cues, which can be crucial for detecting the presence of violence in various settings. 

## **2.1.1 | Methods Based on Audio Features** 

Some methods for violence detection focus on extracting audio features. For example, Cheng et al. [10] utilizded statistical characteristics of audio events over time series to detect semantic contexts such as gunshots, explosions, and car braking. Another method was proposed by Giannakopoulos et al. [11] who employed audio features from time and frequency domains to identify violent scenes. On the other side, Pfeiffer et al. [12] detected aggressive scenes by comparing the amplitude, frequency, and pitch statistics of a window with prerecorded audio in a dataset. This comparison was done either by Euclidean distance or by the correlation of two patterns. Although specific audio events such as explosions and gunshots happen in movies, most surveillance cameras do not support audio recording. Therefore, these methods are less studied nowadays, and most researchers focus on techniques related to visual features. 

## **2.1.2 | Methods Based on Audio-Visual Features** 

These methods employ both audio and visual features for video analysis. For example, Nam et al. [13] proposed a technique that combines audio features with spatio-temporal dynamic activities, flame, and blood detection as visual features. Gong et al. [14] employed low-level audio and visual features to extract the candidate shot from a video sequence. Then, they recognized high-level audio effects such as gunshots and explosions in the extracted candidates. Finally, the candidate shots were categorized into violent or non-violent scenes by integrating probabilistic outputs from previous steps. To improve the performance of other works, Perperis et al. [15] extracted audio and visual features, and then employed a simple meta-classification technique and an ontology-based method for the fusion. The experiments showed that this fusion method outperformed other methods in this field. Another method was proposed by Kooij et al. [16], who utilized audio and visual cues to detect aggressive scenes. A 3D tracking model of a person was employed to extract the features of limb motion. Then, audio information related to screaming and kicking was classified. Finally, a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) [17] was used to fuse the visual and audio cues. 

## **2.1.3 | Methods Based on Visual Features** 

These techniques can be classified into local and global approaches. Local approaches involve extracting interest frames from a video clip and then using a descriptor to represent the video. For example, the authors of [1, 18, 19] employed Motion SIFT (MoSIFT) to detect spatio-temporal interest points. MoSIFT [4] is a technique developed from Scale Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) [20] for obtaining video interest points and features. To improve MoSIFT, Febin et al. [21] proposed Motion Boundary SIFT (MoBSIFT), which extends from MoSIFT and Motion Boundary Histogram (MBH) [22]. De Souza et al. [23] suggested a different approach in which they extracted spatio-temporal features via SIFT and STIP [3]. Then, they exploited the Bag of Visual Words (BoVW) [24] to summarize the extracted features. Finally, their experiments showed that STIP outperformed SIFT for violence detection. Moreover, the authors of [25, 26] also focused on STIP to detect motion. Zhao et al. [25] extracted STIP and introduced a descriptor by combining Histograms of Optical Flow (HOF) and Histograms of Oriented Gradients (HOG). This descriptor was called HNF. Bellamine and Tairi [26] also presented an extended version of STIP named Color Space–Time Interest Points (CSTIP), which described color features around extracted STIP. They then performed motion detection in a video clip using these color features. Despite the effectiveness of local methods in some cases, they may not perform well when there is a high degree of motion in a video, such as in crowded scenes. 

By contrast, global features consider whole frames to address the aforementioned problems. For example, Hassner et al. [2] presented a method based on optical flow magnitude and proposed Violent Flows (ViF) descriptor. Additionally, Gao et al. [27] introduced the Oriented Violent Flows (OViF) descriptor and offered a design that took into consideration the orientation and magnitude of the optical flow. Another descriptor used for detecting human action is the Histogram of Oriented Optical Flow (HOOF). Chaudhry et al. [28] proposed HOOF, which was robust to the scale and direction of the moving person. Indeed, HOOF did not involve human segmentation and background subtraction. Mahmoodi and Salajeghe [29] proposed another method that exploited the magnitude and orientation of the optical flow. They introduced a Histogram of Optical flow Magnitude and Orientation (HOMO). Even though HOMO outperformed state-of-the-art methods in uncrowded scenes, its performance was not satisfactory in crowded scenes. Although the global methods consider all frames to obtain motion information, they are so sensitive to the cluttered background. Therefore, Ben Mabrouk and Zagrouba [30] introduced a descriptor named Distribution of Magnitude and Orientation of Local Interest Frame (DiMOLIF), which combined global and local features. For more information about traditional methods, Ben Mabrouk and Zagrouba [31] wrote a review article on intelligent video surveillance systems, in which they discuss behavioral representations and various models in detail. 

## **2.1.4 | Deep Learning Approaches** 

The capability of deep learning methods to learn intricate data representations has attracted significant attention in recent years. This has led many researchers to explore the use of deep learning

techniques for video analysis tasks, such as object detection, action recognition, and scene understanding. In fact, deep learning methods have shown promising results in various video analysis tasks, and they are now widely used in many applications of intelligent video surveillance systems. For example, Dai et al. [32] utilized a Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) and a CNN to extract spatio-temporal features and long-term dynamic information, respectively. Later, some motion and audio features were employed to make the previous features more robust. Finally, the SVM was used as a classifier after fusing obtained features. Keçeli and Kaya [33] generated various templates from the optical flow magnitude and orientation. These templates were then used to feed a pertained network for feature extraction, which were subsequently classified by an SVM and subspace K-nearest neighbors. Serrano et al. [34] distinguished non-violent from violent scenes using the Hough Forests and a 2D CNN. First, interest points in a frame were extracted using the Features from Accelerated Segment Test (FAST) detector [35]. Then, The Binary Robust Invariant Scalable Keypoints (BRISK) descriptor [36] was exploited to get features around each interest point. Next, these features were sent to Hough Forests to get the weight of the frame. Finally, the sum of weighted frames constructed a 2D template image that fed a 2D CNN. 

3D CNNs have also been used to detect violent behaviors. For example, Ding et al. [37] made an important attempt to discriminate aggressive behaviors using a 3D CNN. In this work, the input of a CNN was all frames, and the output was a label. However, this structure takes more computational time than a 2D CNN since it considers all frames. Therefore, some researchers have proposed algorithms to omit useless frames and consider interest frames as the input of the 3D CNN. For example, Ullah et al. [38] exploited a CNN to detect persons. Persons were detected to extract proper frames, which were sent to a 3D CNN for processing. Moreover, Mahmoodi et al. [39] employed the statistical features of the optical flow to extract interest frames. Then, a 3D CNN was implemented to classify an input video. To address the high computational cost and memory demands associated with 3D CNNs, the authors in [40] presented a dual 3D convolutional network (D3DNet). This new lightweight solution was introduced for real-time video-based action recognition, and it consists of two complementary branches: a coarse branch that quickly maintains a large temporal receptive field and a fine branch that captures multi-resolution spatiotemporal information. 

Some methods consider a multi-stream architecture of CNNs to detect violent behaviors. For example, Mohtavipour et al. [41] used a multi-stream CNN to detect violence. They exploited spatial, temporal, and spatio-temporal streams, which extract features concerning the speed of movement, appearance, and a representative image. Moreover, Feichtenhofer et al. [42] presented a two-stream network architecture for video recognition. They aimed to design a system with the capability of capturing spatial semantics and motion via two pathways. They captured spatial semantics with a slow pathway, which operated at a slow frame rate. Moreover, motion was captured with a fast pathway, which operated at a high frame rate. Therefore, they obtained a strong performance for action classification. Furthermore, Freire-Obregón et al. [43] exploited two CNN streams and an object detection module to improve the performance of the multi-stream CNNs. In Ullah et al. [44], a dual-stream CNN 

framework was employed to detect anomalies in videos. The authors utilized a 2D CNN as an auto-encoder to extract spatial features, which were then sent into an echo state network. 

Other methods exploited CNNs and LSTMs to detect violent behaviors. For example, Abdali and Al-Tuma [45] used VGG19 [46] as a spatial feature extractor. Then, temporal features were extracted by LSTM. Moreover, Vosta and Yow [47] presented a model using a famous CNN (ResNet50) and a ConvLSTM. In fact, ResNet50 was exploited to extract the features of each frame, and a ConvLSTM was used to capture temporal information between frames. In this category, another method has been proposed in Vijeikis et al. [48]. They used the U-Net network followed by an LSTM to extract spatial and temporal features. In Ullah et al. [49], a lightweight ConvLSTM model was utilized to extract relevant information, such as the presence of humans or suspicious objects like knives or guns. These extracted features were subsequently fed into a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) for further processing. 

Our work is based on a 2D CNN with a focus on regions that contain more action. Specifically, we enable a 2D CNN to encode spatio-temporal features. Meaning that we summarize the content of video frames in an image, which is obtained by processing the statistical features of the optical flow magnitude. Then, this image feeds a 2D CNN to distinguish violent from non-violent videos. Finally, we evaluate the effectiveness of our method on four benchmark datasets commonly used in the field of violence detection. These datasets include a wide range of violent and non-violent videos, enabling us to thoroughly test the performance of our method. By comparing our results to state-of-the-art methods, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in improving the accuracy of violent behavior detection in videos. 

## **3 | Proposed Method** 

The proposed method is designed to discriminate between violent and non-violent videos. The method begins by converting the color frames of the video into grayscale format. This conversion is done to simplify the processing and reduce the computational load of the method. 

Once the video frames are in grayscale format, the optical flow magnitude is estimated for two consecutive frames. Optical flow is a technique that estimates the apparent motion of objects in a video sequence by analyzing the changes between consecutive frames. The magnitude of the optical flow for each frame is calculated, and the magnitude changes between the two frames are computed. These magnitude changes are then processed to obtain statistical information about the motion patterns in the video. 

After obtaining the statistical information from the optical flow magnitude changes, a 2D template is used as input to a 2D CNN. This 2D template is designed to capture the motion patterns in the video that are indicative of violent behavior. The 2D CNN is trained on benchmark datasets of labeled videos. Then, input patterns are classified into either violent or nonviolent scenes.

## **3.1 | Feature Extraction** 

Here, we provide a step-by-step explanation of how the statistical features are retrieved from the changes in optical flow magnitude and how the 2D template is created. Overall, the feature extraction process is designed to capture the motion and appearance of violent behaviors in the video in a compact and efficient representation that can be used for classification. 

The first step in the proposed method is to obtain an image from the input video that encodes the motion and appearance of violent behaviors. This image is used as input to a 2D CNN for classification. To create this image, the method employs a process that involves converting the color frames of the video to grayscale format and then extracting statistical features from the changes in optical flow magnitude. 

The conversion of color frames to grayscale is done to simplify the processing and reduce the computational load of the method. Grayscale images have a single channel, which makes them easier to process and can help to reduce the complexity. 

The next step is to retrieve statistical features from the changes in optical flow magnitude. The magnitude of the optical flow for each frame is calculated, and the magnitude changes between two consecutive frames are computed. These magnitude changes are then processed to obtain statistical features that capture the motion patterns in the video. Examples of statistical features that can be extracted include range filter, standard deviation, and entropy.

The last three fully connected layers have 500, 250, and 2 neurons, respectively. We also incorporate dropout in the architecture of the 2D CNN. The dropout is used either to reduce overfitting or to improve the generalization of the network. The final layer of the 2D CNN is a softmax classification layer, which is exploited for the classification task. 

## **3.2 | Convolutional Neural Network** 

The CNN consists of sequential layers, starting with an input layer that is connected to the convolutional and pooling layers, followed by the output layer. Specifically, a CNN extracts features from the input data that are then passed on to the fully connected layers for classification. In this article, a simple CNN is used to discriminate violent from non-violent videos. The CNN architecture consists of three convolutional layers followed by max-pooling layers, and the last three layers are fully connected. The suggested CNN architecture is shown in Figure 4. 

As shown in Figure 4, the input of the network is an image. The output is a label that shows whether an input image is violent or non-violent. If a scene is detected as a violent video, the output will be one. Otherwise, it will be zero. 

The network architecture consists of three convolutional layers followed by a batch normalization layer and a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. Batch normalization is used to improve the performance and stability of the network [51]. Since we want to extract local information in the 2D templates, the kernel size of convolutional layers is set to 3 × 3. In fact, bigger kernels are useful for extracting global and high-level information. Actually, we want to increase the ability of the proposed pipeline for analyzing crowded scenes where violent behaviors occur in small regions of a scene. Moreover, max-pooling is employed at the end of each convolutional layer to perform down-sampling. Generally, there are three types of pooling operations, which are max pooling, min pooling, and average pooling. As we want to extract salient regions derived from much more action, we exploit max pooling. In fact, the statistical features summarized in the 2D template reflect objects with much more action, and the higher pixel values show more motion. ## **5 | Conclusion** 

In this article, we suggest a useful technique to identify violent behaviors in video scenes. Our approach involves summarizing a video clip into a 2D template, which is then fed into a 2D CNN for classification. The statistical features of optical flow changes are exploited to obtain the 2D template. These features are designed to draw attention to regions with more action. In fact, our proposed method has a simple architecture with fewer parameters compared to 3D CNNs. As a result, it is easier to implement on hardware than 3D CNNs. We also demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method, which utilizes efficient statistical features and a 2D CNN, through the experimental results in both crowded and uncrowded scenes. It provides the accuracy of 98.54% ± 0.99% and 100% on the Hockey Fight and Violent Flows dataset, respectively. For the Surveillance Fights and Action Movies datasets, we obtain accuracy rates of 94.19% ± 4.22% and 100%, respectively. We hope to introduce new deep neural network topologies for violence detection in upcoming efforts.
