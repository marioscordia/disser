## **SmarterROUTES—A Data-driven Context-aware Solution for Personalized Dynamic Routing and Navigation** 

JELLE DE BOCK and STEVEN VERSTOCKT, Ghent University–imec, IDLab, Belgium 

SmarterROUTES contributes to personalised routing and navigation by data-driven route ranking and an environmentally aware road scene complexity-estimation mechanism. Traditional routing algorithms provide the fastest, shortest, or most ecological route by calculating the lowest-cost path from A to B using an underlying network of weighted connections. Our implementation goes a step further and ranks a set of such routes. The ranking is the result of additional weighing based on governmental data sets, extracts of the OpenStreetMap (OSM) database, or periodically adapted extracts of web services (e.g., Representational state transfer APIs). The selected data sets and their relative contributions to the overall ranking mechanism can be dynamically adapted by the end-user. Another major contribution toward a fully personalised navigation experience is the implementation of a road scene complexity scoring mechanism. Road complexity is estimated based on the combined input from geospatial data sources, traffic data, sensor analysis, and Street View–based complexity analysis. The latter input source uses Street View images as input for a Densenet Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), pre-trained on buildings using Bag-of-Words and Structure-from-motion features, outputting an image descriptor. The current version uses an adapted version of this network to predict road complexity scores. The predicted values correspond with the subjective complexity judgements of the end-users.

Car manufacturers have made a lot of effort to improve driving assistance. First, we look at existing routing implementations and address the major limitation, which is the inability of the _ad hoc_ inclusion of extra routing criteria. Several excellent routing algorithms are already very capable at providing a user some kind of customised routes. Section 2 provides a brief overview of these semi-personalised routing algorithms. Unlike the traditional graph-based approach, our method analyses and ranks route candidates based on a collection of geospatially annotated properties (e.g., a json collection of points-of-interest of a certain type). Subsequently, we contribute toward personalised driving assistance by proposing a road scene complexity mechanism (Section 3.3). ## **2 RELATED WORK** 

The challenges we try to tackle, personalised routing and navigation assistance, were already subjected to thorough research previously. In the following subsections, we look at several interesting approaches for personalised routing and navigation. 

## **2.1 Personalised Routing** 

For large-scale road networks, traditional shortest-path routing algorithms, such as A* and Dijkstra, are insufficient, as they have calculation times that are a function of their network size [24]. Practically usable routing algorithms try to use knowledge of their road network to improve their search efficiency and mostly follow a two-stage process. First, there is some kind of pre-processing, during which heuristics are used to pre-calculate a part of the graph connections. This process can take somewhere between some minutes and several hours (Delling et al. [7], Bast et al. [3]). In a second stage, the real-time route calculation algorithms provide the user with a route that is satisfying his/her requirements and is minimising some kind of cost function. 

Routing algorithms are also further distinguished from each other based on their characteristics or their use-cases. The first characteristic is whether it supports single or multiple costs. As mentioned, commercially available route providers usually consider a handful of cost metrics such as travel time, distance and delay along the route. Closely related to this, time dependence is another important characteristic for a routing algorithm. An optimal route generated at 7 a.m. might be completely sub optimal at 1 p.m. Another criteria we can set is how well the algorithm copes with uncertainty. This last criterion also includes personalisation. A nervous driver might want another route than a casual driver who does not mind a bit of delay. In this article, we want to further focus on personalised routing. We start by introducing the already existing approaches for personalised routing followed by a short introduction to our own methodology to tackle personalised routing. 

_2.1.1 Stochastic Routing._ Stochastic on-time arrival (SOTA) approaches are considering route planning as finding the best possible path from source to destination given a limited budget (time). Travel times can also be stochastic, meaning that the time to complete a certain segment is variable. Several approaches to solve this planning problem exist. First, the shortest path with on-time arrival (SPOTAR), is a proposed algorithm by Wu [33], based on first-order dominance of the path, but is resulting in a worst case exponential time complexity. Based on the original implementation, improved SOTA algorithms were developed, but they are still rather time consuming to be implemented in real time navigation appliances [42]. 

_2.1.2 Pareto-optimal Routing._ Pareto-optimal routing is another approach to fulfil the emerging urge for route personalisation. They use a principle called skyline routing to provide users with a set of routes to choose from that are satisfying a number of their preferences. The skyline route is the one of all the available ones that is at least as good or better than any other routes. Kriegel et al. [26] proposed a route-querying algorithm that was able to calculate user-preferable routes in a multi-attribute road network. They use a preference vector for the importance of each of the attributes in the road network graph. When multiple criteria are available, the skyline can exist of multiple routes [48]. The more criteria available, the more routes to choose from, which is making the intuitive presentation of the routes to the user an important aspect to consider. In another publication, Kriegel et al. [15] implemented a visual demonstrator to present users’ with pareto-optimal routes based on a maximum of three road attributes. They also stated that for presentation purposes no more than three attributes should be used to keep the route browsing intuitive and user-friendly and as otherwise there would be too many skyline routes to choose from. This approach provides the user with a route that corresponds to their preference vectors as

_2.1.3 Customisable Route Planning._ Customisable route planning is a sub challenge within routing in which we want real-time route querying with user-level personalisable and customisable parameters. It is a dynamic route planning technique used to efficiently update path costs and is especially useful for updating the road network with dynamic, real-time metrics (Funke and Strorandt [14]). Dai et al. [6] suggested a personalised route recommendation methodology that modelled driving preferences based on a user’s historical trajectories. They first compiled a graph based on the combination of users’ preferences and trajectories. The graph was compiled based on a set of historical travelled paths, accompanied with start/endpoint, departure time and user preferences. The route that is recommended is the one with lowest arc cost in the generated graph. Funke and Strorandt investigated how to speed-up the previous approach. They suggested a hybrid approach. A part of the customisation happened during edge cost calculation (and updating), another part took place during the actual path querying phase. All of the above is illustrating that nowadays great steps toward fully customised routing were made. Pre-processing has been reduced to the bare minimum, but most of the suggested techniques still use some kind of precalculation, which is making full and true individual user customisation rather time consuming. 

_2.1.4 Preference Mining Based on Historical Trajectories._ Baltenau et al. [2] concluded that the generated route by commercial route services (e.g., Google Maps, Bing Maps or TomTom) often didn’t correspond to a route that a user actually would prefer. They proposed a solution that consisted of two cost types. The first type of costs were the traditional static path-related ones (e.g., distance, travel time and number of red lights). A second set of costs was generated by analysis and selection from the preference distributions of a user’s historical trajectory data. Their routing solution was now a combination of the conventional shortest-path calculation, which should also fulfill the additional user requirements. 

Yang et al. [49] and Delling et al. [8] suggested methodologies to calculate edge costs based on user preferences gathered from historical trajectories. Apart from traditional edge costs (e.g., travel time, distance) other more context-related costs (e.g., weather, time of the day, driver stress level) can be derived from historical traces. Hu et al. [22] concluded that user preference modelling should also distinguish between risk-loving and risk-averse drivers. For instance, a narrow traveltime distribution will be very suitable for risk-averse drivers. Although the travel time might not be optimal in all of the cases, the driver has an almost guaranteed estimated time of arrival. 

Guo et al. [16] diverged from the previous implementations by using a trajectory set for the actual path calculation. They generate a graph-like structure based on a user’s historical trajectories as their routing infrastructure. They first cluster the route graph in regions. These are the areas in the entire network that have a dense historical trajectory set. It is possible that a startand endpoint have no direct historical trajectory. If this is the case, then they calculate a route that is visiting a number of regions on its path. The path between region pairs is calculated with help of the preferences learned from the densely populated regions along the route. 

Another interesting approach that also considers weighted users’ preferences in the routing process was presented by Zeng et al. [50]. They introduced a specific method to solve the keyword coverage problem. All nodes in the routing graph are labelled to which extent it satisfies a certain need. The implementation finds an optimal route from a source location to a target location such that the fulfilment of a user’s need is optimised and that the budget score (i.e., the sum of arc weights) falls within a specified constraint. Conventional route calculation services often only consider a limited amount of routing options. However, it has been shown that there are also less obvious aspects that make navigation challenging. As illustrated by Sladewski et al. [44], road turns and the accompanied road complexity are a good example of less apparent characteristics that make navigation challenging. _2.1.5 SmarterROUTES Personalised Routing Approach._ As introduced in the previous paragraphs, most of the personalised routing solutions use pre-processing to build a road network graph suited for personalised querying. With SmarterROUTES, we approach personalised routing in a post-processing manner. Our methodology uses the provided routes of existing algorithms and applies post-processing to them. As most existing routing algorithms provide the top _N_ bestmatching routes, we can use these routes and their via-points as input for our methodology. Route points are post-processed by an additional weighting and scoring mechanism based on multiple geospatial datasets and the users’ preferences. Furthermore, instead of using complex graph algorithms (as for instance proposed by Zeng and Hendawi), however, our approach follows the KISS (Keep it simple, stupid—and Keep it short, sustainable) principle and uses a recursive scoring mechanism that iteratively improves the route (generated by a route provider) by adding “via points” to the best ranked route(s). In Section 3, we will further explain the details of this weighting and ranking mechanism. 

As a final note, we want to make clear that our proposed solution is not replacing the discussed (semi-)personalised routing algorithms but it is rather an extension on the already existing implementations. For instance, when an additional weighting happens on the client side, additional preferences can easily be included or omitted by simply adding or removing it from the weighting function. This might result in a decrease in the required degree of personalisation in the current routing algorithms, which might decrease the number of graph rebuilds or overall pre-processing time. 

## **2.2 Personalised Data-driven Navigation** 

Once a personalised route is available, the user will try to navigate its way to the destination using the route instructions. Routing instructions are calculated based on a route that is mapped on a known road network. The maneuvers that should be performed at a given location are usually presented audio-visually on navigation devices. In some circumstances, however, the navigation instructions are rather high-level and abstract, especially at difficult intersections on the navigation path [9]. The finding that users prefer landmark-based navigation when complexity increases can be incorporated in the SmarterROUTES implementation by utilisation of meta-information (e.g., does a given intersection has traffic lights or is there a bus stop nearby) from geospatial data sources to provide personalised and context-aware routing and navigation. 

Another factor that is often neglected is that technology and navigation assistance have evolved over the years. A decade ago most users had a standalone GPS device, often permanently installed in the vehicle. Nowadays, modern GPS devices tend to have connectivity with smartphones and the internet or the navigation service itself is even entirely integrated in the user’s smartphone. Another factor that might be beneficial for navigation purposes is the maturation of Heads-UpDisplays (HUDs). A study of Morris et al. [30] pointed out that when driving, a distraction of as little as 2 s significantly increases the risk of having an accident. Furthermore, they also found out that the average distraction caused by glancing at the navigation device is longer than any other distraction whilst driving. From one side, HUDs take away the need to glance away from the road for navigation assistance, but from the other side, as the data is continually in sight of the user, the possibility of distraction by data overload still exists and should be carefully managed. Complexity driven data management that can be integrated in HUDs could be an interesting approach to help achieve optimal distraction management. 

To estimate the complexity of an intersection, turn, or road segment, several interesting features are discussed in literature. Hochmair and Fu [21], for example, suggest using bicycle-car interactions based on data from a bicycle suitability map. Such maps, however, are mostly focused around one particular area or need to be constructed/estimated based on features from Geographic Information System (GIS) datasets (such as OSM and Waze). Another, complementary approach, is to use images of the environment to facilitate navigation and to estimate the complexity, such as the landmark-based instructions proposed by Hile et al. [19]. SmarterROUTES tries to use a combination of image processing, sensor analysis, and GIS data (OpenStreetMap) to give a representative indication about the overall complexity for a certain road scene (see Section 3.1) contributing to optimal road distraction management caused by navigation. 

## **3 CONTEXT-AWARE AND USER-CENTERED ROUTING AND NAVIGATION** 

The methodologies proposed in this article contribute to the user routing and navigation process in two important ways. First, a route-ranking mechanism is introduced that further optimises the routes that were generated by popular routing platforms (using their own underlying road networks). This weighting process renders acceptable routing candidates (since they are generated by a well established routing provider and the underlying algorithm), which are then further personalised by a thoughtful use of weighting and scoring mechanisms powered by a broad and extendable collection of open data. 

Consequently, the need for personalised navigation was carefully investigated. A contribution toward personalised and context-aware navigation was made by the introduction of a complexity scoring mechanism. This mechanism might on its turn serve as input for a “smart” navigation implementation, which considers user-specific and context-related knowledge and is capable of providing a Level-of-Detail-aware, context-adaptable navigation experience.

## **3.1 User-preference-based Data-driven Route Ranking** 

Nowadays, with the rise of affordable mobile data and its extended coverage area offloading route calculation and generation to a back-end service is a justified approach [13] (see step 1, Figure 1). This mechanism provides several advantages over how traditional GPS devices handled this calculation. Back in the days, navigation equipment only had GPS-connectivity and thus was unable to pull in online data. The first advantage of using back-end connectivity is that the route calculation can be made highly dynamic. Real-time weather conditions, traffic light statuses, and many other sources of relevant contextual information can be considered during route calculation and navigation. Another plus side is that route calculation can be highly personalised with the inclusion of use-case-specific data sets. To fully unlock the true potential of this data-driven route calculation mechanism relevant data sets and their underlying features should be dynamically selected. This selection process should ideally happen by the end-user, giving them the ability to focus on aspects they favour during the ranking of the pre-calculated routes. Data can be either static (e.g., ancient castles or speed bumps) or dynamic (e.g., weather or traffic jams). The former type of data should be updated far less frequently than the latter. 

The achievement of the proposed idea of route suggestion based on custom data sets (see Figure 1) should be fulfilled by the unification of various data sources and types. This means that all data sets should be adapted or translated to a common format which is understood by the data manager of the mechanism. After thorough examination of the various available options, geojson[2] was selected for this purpose. The format guarantees easy import and export and is significantly facilitating both calculation and presentation of routing data (in comparison with a raw data file or

As previously mentioned, the types of data used for data-driven route ranking can be basically divided in two sub types. The major source of information is static and is often the result of processing open data made available by governmental institutions. Such releases usually take place once a year or month and are often themed around a certain topic. In the current (and initial) implementation of the ranking mechanism the available static data originates from querying and processing specific features (e.g., buildings, tunnels, petrol stations, road surfaces or land use) of the OSM database with the Overpass Query Language and from the processing and conversion of the aforementioned governmental data sets (criminality per municipality or road accidents per municipality). Furthermore, the route selection mechanism also uses dynamic data such as weather data (millimetres of precipitation) which is regularly (e.g., hourly) retrieved and converted to geojson from a publicly available web service. 

It is worth mentioning that the above data sources are just an initial data selection and in future iterations or specific use cases the current collection of data can be easily extended or modified by simply feeding additional geojson files to the data-manager (see Figure 1). 

_Intelligent data selection facilitated by data structuring and clustering_ . Once the data set is geojson formatted the next step is the discovery of paths leading to useful properties in the geojson data sets (see “weighting mechanism,” Figure 1). In the current implementation the property management is done by human reasoning and manual selection. In future iterations and improvements of the architecture this property selection process should ideally happen (semi)automatically. This means, when a new dataset is presented, the system automatically detects if the data is geospatially annotated. If this is the case, then it searches for recurring parameters in the dataset (e.g., rainfall in millimeters or accidents per 1,000 cars) and automatically detects datatype and ranges within that dataset. A big step toward this goal could be the implementation of a json path management platform using the principles as described by Bourhis et al. [4]. 

When an interesting data set was found and loaded in the SmarterROUTES platform (i.e., the data-manager that handles this task, Figures 1 and 2), the properties relevant for routing and navigation are retrieved and registered. With the available collection of useful properties, we are now able to make a selection of useful properties for a certain use case or user group. One property, although it might be a relevant route-ranking property, might not have the same importance for everybody. A car driver will perhaps look at precipitation in a different way than a cyclist might do. To realise a personalised route suggestion mechanism the data powering the weighing of the routes should be first collected and normalised. The current route-ranking mechanism achieves this task by generating a configuration that can be fully customised. A configuration consists of the selected properties from the available collection of data sets (see Figure 2). _Generation process of routes for data-driven ranking_ . Road users nowadays can rely on a cluster of technological aids during their trip. To help them find directions they often use generated routes (A to B, or round-trips). The actual routing mechanism is not covered in this publication but generally routes are calculated by weighing road segments and by selecting the best (or the n best) route from a start- to an endpoint. This is typically the road with the lowest total weight. For this purpose algorithms like Dijkstra’s algorithm [25] and A* search [34] are used as explained in detail by Nha et al. [32]. 

A lot of commercial and open source routing calculation services do exist and some of them do a very good job. For the SmarterROUTES platform (see Figure 1) a commercial routing service (TomTom) was selected for this initial route calculation task. TomTom has a large user base and is therefore very accurate in predicting actual live traffic (users with TomTom app and TomTom navigation devices send anonymous traffic data). Additionally, running a custom routing service needs an underlying road network, which should be accurate and always up-to-date to guarantee safe and reliable routes. This is an example of a time- and resource-consuming aspect that can be easily offloaded to an external provider with the aid of modern technology and connectivity. As a bonus, TomTom users do have the ability to report mapping issues. Those reported glitches are periodically corrected by TomTom. Another factor to select a commercial provider is the cost of running and maintaining a routing service. In short, the use of an out-of-the-box routing solution allows more focus on the actual scoring-, data-selection-, and LOD-mechanisms.

_Data-driven route ranking: bringing routes and data sets together_ . The ranking is provided as a single score, re-scaled from 0 to 10. In this procedure, the scoring mechanism is implemented based on a set of weights (line 1, Algorithm 1) and scoring mechanism (line 2, Algorithm 1). The former is a set of weights for each of the scoring sub mechanisms and is either set by users themselves or is preset for a certain user group. The latter is a separate procedure (an JavaScript implemented example is shown in Appendix A). These types of procedures loop over the available route points (contained in the _route_ variable, line 5, Algorithm 1) and provide a score based on a given criteria (e.g., total meters climbed). Each parameter has a certain configuration (see Figure 2), so all of the parameter-based subscores are weighed to give them the desired positive or negative impact on the final global score. The route with the best final score is considered to be the best route, given a certain parameter configuration. The first set of four deviation points are generated by finding a point on the road network closest to the theoretically calculated point situated at ±45[◦] of start or end point and one-fourth of the distance between start and midpoint (or end and midpoint) away. In total, eight alternatives are generated. The alternatives are still in the neighbourhood of the initially selected route (the one that was selected for improvement). It is worth mentioning that the mechanism on its own is not suggesting alternative routes that are by definition “better” then the previous route and is only generating new route candidates that are slight variations on the original route. It is the role of the weighting and ranking mechanism to recalculate and rank the scores of both the alternative routes and the original route. If the original route (i.e., the one without any detours) is still better than any of the alternatives, then the user can still stick with the original route. 

## **3.2 Level-of-Detail-driven Data Management** 

With our straightforward route-ranking system, a user might have selected a route optimally corresponding to its needs. In this section, we try to make another major contribution toward user-centred navigation and driving. As introduced in the related work section of this article, various aspects are complicating driving a vehicle. The combination of the state of the road infrastructure, the driving maneuvers to execute and traffic or weather circumstances on the driver’s route are determining the complexity of the driving process. In the related work section (see Section 2) [10, 29, 38, 40], we already discussed some general principles of how users interact

Subsequently, if we truly want to centre navigation around the individual, then we have to ask the following question: “How much and at which moment do users want to see the relevant (navigation) information?” To answer this exact question two main things should be considered. The first aspect is that not all users are the same. One driver might be obsessed by data and might be interested in every single detail whilst driving. Another one might want the least information possible and thus letting the navigational device provide just enough information to orient themselves. In a user-centred navigational approach this choice should ideally be left over to the users themselves. As Cellario [5] pointed out, another important aspect of data management is the huge amount of new available data that might cause an information overload for drivers. The U.S. Department of Transportation has made considerable efforts in this field of study. They concluded that information should be checked against the following criteria: information type; priority and complexity; trip status and driving load; and the driver profile [23]. Although the latter two criteria are undeniably very important to the safety of an intelligent navigation system, we will focus on the former aspect of data filtering. A mechanism to estimate environmental complexity based on available road-related data will be discussed to provide a clear indication on how much attention on the road might be needed given a certain road scene. 

## **3.3 Scoring an Environment’s Complexity** 

Before we introduce the various components of our suggested complexity estimation mechanism, we will first focus on what complexity actually means in our context. The term complexity was originally derived from the Latin word _complexus_ , which meant “weaving things together” [43]. In 2003, Ottino [36] came with a description for complex systems, which are considered complex if a lot of individual components and interactions exist. As a lot of maneuvers and interactions with other drivers and the road infrastructure take place whilst driving, it is acceptable to consider the driving process with its accompanied traffic as a complex system. Schlindwein and Ison [43] concluded that we can best describe or measure complexity by dividing the possible indicators in one of the two subcategories; descriptive or perceived complexity. Descriptive measurements are those that can be quantitatively and subjectively measured. Some examples are cars on the road, the distance between them or the number of speed bumps. The other category, perceived complexity, tries to valorize the objective measurement of the user’s perception. When we apply this perceived complexity on our driving context, we want to translate the thoughts of a driver whilst riding a certain road segment (e.g., this looks dangerous or this sector is badly illuminated or I can barely see the road) into a single value. Combination and prioritisation (i.e., weighting) of various measurements of both categories into a single score will provide an intelligent driving assistance system with the ultimate input about the current road scene’s complexity. 

_Perceived complexity score: Convolutional Neural Net Image Retrieval (CIR)_ . Perceived complexity is in fact the user’s objective measurement of a road scene’s complexity. One would think that humans are quite capable to visually judge the inherent risk of a certain road situation. Although this might be true to a certain extent, studies on traffic accidents causes show that the major part of incidents are caused by human mistakes [41]. These findings makes the choice to use a combination of multiple descriptive measurements with the proposed perceived complexity estimation mechanism obvious. We will first discus perceived complexity quantification and introduce a solution based on the framework of Radenović et al. [37]. This framework performs an especially good job in finding similar images. It does so by intelligent selection of descriptor features (Figure 5).

## **5 CONCLUSION** 

The main objectives of the SmarterROUTES approach are end-user customisation and keeping routing and navigation relatively simple to use and implement. This article presents two contributions to a fully personalised routing and navigation platform. First, this article proposes an approach to customise default route calculation by a data-driven route-ranking system. The ranking mechanism provides the user with a customised ordering of the traditionally generated number of alternative routes (with respect to a user’s preferences). This route can be converted into the GPX-file sharing format to use it for navigation. 

The second contribution toward personalised routing and navigation is the implementation of a complexity estimation model. This can be considered as a first and important step toward userdriven and Level-of-Detail-aware navigation assistance. 

As shown in the results section the coverage of geospatial metadata such as land-use and road surface are highly region dependent. Additional geospatial data sets or data augmentation
