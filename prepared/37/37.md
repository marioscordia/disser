## **Abstract** 

This paper explores the practical implementation of 3D Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for real-time violence detection in surveillance scenarios, focusing on applications such as traffic aggression and bullying detection. In recent years, the proliferation of surveillance cameras has provided a vast amount of visual data, necessitating efficient and accurate automated methods for threat identification. In this study, we investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of employing 3D CNNs for violence detection in reallife scenarios. We propose a comprehensive framework that integrates data augmentation techniques and fine-tuning strategies to address the challenges of limited annotated data and diverse environmental conditions. Furthermore, we conduct extensive experiments on benchmark datasets to evaluate the performance of the proposed approach in detecting physical altercations. In summary, this study highlights the efficacy of 3D CNNs in detecting physical altercations, thereby contributing to the advancement of automated surveillance systems aimed at enhancing public safety. The findings presented herein not only underscore the practical utility of deep learning techniques in addressing real-world challenges but also point towards future avenues for improving the accuracy and reliability of violence detection algorithms through multimodal integration.

## **2. State of the Art** 

There are numerous studies conducted on violence detection, including the LHOG and LHOF Case Study, CNN and LSTM Case Study, and 3D CNN. 

- An example for **LHOG and LHOF Case Study** is in [1], where optical flow field distribution is utilized for motion region segmentation. Within these regions, two types of low-level features are extracted to represent violent behaviors’ appearance and dynamics. The two methods are the Local Histogram of Oriented Gradient (LHOG) from RGB images and the Local Histogram of Optical Flow (LHOF) from optical flow images. These features are encoded using the Bag of Words (BoW) model to eliminate redundant information and generate a specific length vector for each video. Subsequently, the video vectors are classified using Support Vector Machine (SVM) [1]. The process involves video preprocessing, motion region segmentation, feature extraction, feature processing, and prediction. Similar approaches are presented in [2, 3]. 

- The architecture described in [4] utilizes a CNN for feature extraction, followed by a **long short-term memory network** (LSTM) for sequence prediction based on the extracted characteristic vectors. The Xception network architecture, pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset [4], was chosen for this purpose. By leveraging the pre-trained model, resources and time are saved as only the last 4 layers are retrained while the initial layers remain unchanged. The resulting model achieves a classification time of approximately 7.89 milliseconds per frame, making it well-suited for real-time violence detection. Similar approaches are presented in [5, 6]. 

- Another approach to identifying violence involves utilizing a **3D CNN** as outlined in reference [7]. This method is capable of recognizing intricate sequential patterns to forecast violence with a high level of precision. Initially, the camera captures the video stream, which is then inputted directly into a MobileNet CNN model that has been trained to identify individuals. Upon detecting a person in the video, a sequence of 16 frames is sent to the 3D CNN model to extract spatiotemporal characteristics. These characteristics are subsequently passed to a Softmax classifier for a comprehensive analysis and prediction. If violence is detected, an alert is dispatched to the nearest security department. Similar approaches are presented in [8, 9, 10].

## _3.2. Data Preparation_ 

The final dataset comprises a total of 1, 446 videos, which are categorized into violent and non-violent. Each category consists of 723 videos, and the total is evenly divided. We have saved the file path for these videos. The distribution for training, validation, and testing is as follows: 70%, 20%, and 10% respectively. For training the model, we utilized 70% of the dataset, which amounts to 506 videos from each category. The model was then validated using

We have employed a script that converts the videos into frames, resulting in the creation of folders named after the respective videos (e.g., _video_ . _avi_ or _video_ . _mpg_ ). Each frame within these folders is numbered from 1. _jpg_ to the maximum number of possible frames. Subsequently, _the frames are organized into stacks_ . Each stack consists of 16 frames. ## _3.3. Data Preprocessing_ 

The stack’s images are initially resized to 128×171 pixels, then square-cropped to 112×112 pixels to align with the 3D CNN’s input shape. Subsequently, the image is transformed into a PyTorch tensor, and each RGB frame is normalized using _mean_ = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and _std_ = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]. This standard transformation technique is based on the normalization method from ImageNet [15]. Figure 8 depicts the preprocessing and normalization steps. Following the creation of the stacks, they were saved as _train_ . _h_ 5, _val_ . _h_ 5, and _test_ . _h_ 5 files.

## _3.4. The Proposed Model_ 

_The performance of 3D CNN_ has served as the inspiration for the proposed model. This network is comprised of eight convolutional layers, five max-pooling layers, and two fully connected layers, culminating in a sigmoidal output layer. The convolutional layers utilize 3 × 3 × 3 kernels with a stride, while the max pooling layers employ 2 × 2 × 2 kernel sizes, except for the initial layer which uses 1 × 2 × 2, with two strides to retain time-based information. The number of filters in each convolutional layer is 64 for the first, 128 for the second and 256 for the third layer. The fully connected layers, _fc_ 6, and _fc_ 7, consist of 4, 096 neurons, and the Sigmoid layer produces two outputs: violent - 1 and non-violent - 0. ## _3.5. Optimization of Hyperparameters_ 

_Grid Search_ [11] is a method used to _find the best hyperparameter values_ for a model through an exhaustive search on specific parameter values. The model also referred to as an estimator, is fine-tuned by exploring various combinations of hyperparameters. To determine the optimal hyperparameters for our model, we conducted a Grid Search on the number of epochs, batch size, learning rate, and dropout layer threshold. After spending 21, 899 seconds, which is approximately 6 hours, on this search, we identified the most suitable hyperparameters: 10 epochs, 30 batch size, 0.0001 learning rate, and 0.5 dropout layer threshold. 

## _3.6. Final Model_ 

The ultimate design showcases a 3D CNN structure consisting of eight convolutional layers, five max-pooling layers, two fully connected layers, and one sigmoidal layer. The API hosted in an EC2 instance allows for _universal access to the model_ . There are two distinct endpoints available: one for uploading videos and another for utilizing the model itself. The video upload endpoint utilizes the AWS Lambda function to preprocess the data, store the frames in the DunamoDB database, and provide the user with an identification number. Subsequently, the user can invoke the second endpoint using the provided ID to execute the model on the video. The endpoint will then respond with the percentage of violence in the video. ## _4.2.1. Testing the Model on Videos_ 

We decided to conduct a series of tests on multiple videos[1] in order _gain a deeper understanding of the factors that lead the model to classify frames_ as violent. Each video was examined, and whenever individuals were detected using YOLO v8n, we saved 16 frames in a list to be utilized by the violence detection model. Once the 16 frames were saved, the initial 8 frames were removed, and the subsequent 8 frames became the next frames in the video sequence. Following a comprehensive analysis, we made the following discoveries: 

- **The man assaults a child**[2] . In this video, there is a clear depiction of a man assaulting a child. Both individuals, a man and a child, are visible in the footage. The man forcefully grabs the child’s sweatshirt, constituting an act of assault. Due to the abrupt physical contact, the model is capable of identifying violence with an accuracy rate of 80%. 

- **The sea**[3] . A woman looking at the sea is correctly classified as nonviolent. 

- **Activities**[4] . Several people are being filmed, and the frames change quite quickly, but the model can classify them as non-violent. What is interesting about this video is the moment when a boy hits a ball hard with a baseball bat, as it is classified as violent. This is because the model perceives sudden movements as violent. 

- **Abuse**[5] . The video depicts a man mistreating a woman. The model does not categorize the man’s threats as violent when he raises his hand as if to strike her but identifies the violence when the man physically lashes out at the woman. 

- **People walking**[6] . This video is categorized as violent due to the presence of numerous crowds. The model’s classification of videos is not consistently accurate when there is an abundance of people standing closely together in the frame, as it fails to differentiate between aggressive crowds and peaceful gatherings. 

These are merely a few of the video experiments we have conducted, yet we believe they hold significant relevance. _The purpose of these experiments was to showcase the model’s capability_ to identify violence in diverse contexts beyond its original training. Remarkably, the model exhibited a high level of accuracy in correctly categorizing violent scenes. 

## _4.2.2. Model Testing on Trailers_ 

The model was tested on movie trailers to determine _the minimum number of violent sequences required for the film to be labeled as violent_ . It is important to note that each sequence consists of 16 frames. Since trailers serve as a preview of the movie, they should contain enough violent scenes to give viewers an idea of what to expect. The methodology employed was similar to the video experiment, where frames were fed into the violence detection model only when individuals were detected by the YOLO v8n model. Upon conducting a detailed analysis, the following findings were uncovered: 

- **Kill Bill**[7] . In this trailer, the model detects 95 violent sequences and 212 non-violent sequences. This means that roughly 30% of the trailer consists of violent sequences. 

- **Slap Shot**[8] . The model detects 114 violent sequences and 182 non-violent sequences, which means that 38% of the video consists of violent sequences. 

- **The Karate Kid**[9] . In the trailer, the model detects 114 violent sequences and 228 non-violent sequences, so 33% of the content is violent.

## _4.2.3. Training the Model on a Di_ ff _erent Dataset_ 

We tried to train the model on the **RWF-2000** dataset. This dataset _contains_ 2, 000 _videos extracted from surveillance cameras_ with real scenes from all over the world and collected from YouTube. The accuracy of the 3D CNN model on the RWF-2000 data set in reference [16] is 87%. Unfortunately, fast movements can be misinterpreted, especially if the quality of the frames is not the best. 

_The model could not be trained using this dataset_ due to insufficient computational resources, as the videos consist of an average of 17 frames, which is significantly higher compared to the 8-10 frames in the other three datasets. Additionally, the dataset contains numerous irrelevant frames that consume a large amount of memory and do not contribute to the effective training of the model. 

## **5. Conclusion** 

The model created and presented in this paper is based on a 3D convolutional neural network that identifies physical violence. This model was trained on several databases. To have relevant data the videos were replaced with frames over which the necessary preprocessing was applied. The model was adjusted following a Grid Search with the most optimal hyperparameters, and the accuracy of the model is 87.25%. The model was tested on several videos, and the frames were sent to the model only if at least one person was detected with YOLO v9. 

In conclusion, _the proposed violence detection model is suitable for practical use due to its high accuracy_ . The process involves three steps: (1) identifying individuals with a pre-trained YOLO v8n model, (2) preprocessing frames after reaching a count of 16, and (3) utilizing a 3D CNN model for violence detection. This model’s ability to recognize violence stems from its capacity to learn intricate pattern sequences. Upon analyzing predictions in the final stage, decisions are made. We are confident that this model can effectively identify and flag violence in surveillance camera footage. While the primary objective has been met, there is potential for enhancement to prevent false alarms. 

## _5.1. Future Work_ 

_Violence detection poses a significant challenge_ due to its intricate nature and high computational demands. The current model we have developed can be enhanced by employing a work environment that aligns with the complexity of this issue. In the future, if computational resources become more accessible, we can create a new dataset for training the model. This dataset would consist of the _three datasets we have utilized thus far, along with a fourth dataset called RWF_ . To _optimize RWF_ , it is crucial to eliminate unnecessary frames where individuals are not present, thereby conserving memory. By incorporating a larger dataset, we can encompass more instances of violence, enabling the model to identify such situations with even greater precision. Additionally, _augmenting the model with sound_ can further aid in the detection of violent scenarios.
