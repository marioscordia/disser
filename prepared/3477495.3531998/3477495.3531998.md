## **ABSTRACT** 

The truncation of ranking lists predicted by retrieval models is vital to ensure users’ search experience. Particularly, in specific vertical domains where documents are usually complicated and extensive (e.g., legal cases), the cost of browsing results is much higher than traditional IR tasks (e.g., Web search) and setting a reasonable cutoff position is quite necessary. While it is straightforward to apply existing result list truncation approaches to legal case retrieval, the effectiveness of these methods is limited because they only focus on simple document statistics and usually fail to capture the context information of documents in the ranking list. These existing efforts also treat result list truncation as an isolated task instead of a component in the entire ranking process, limiting the usage of truncation in practical systems. To tackle these limitations, we propose LeCut, a ranking list truncation model for legal case retrieval. LeCut utilizes contextual features of the retrieval task to capture the semantic-level similarity between documents and decides the best cut-off position with attention mechanisms. We further propose a Joint Optimization of Truncation and Reranking (JOTR) framework based on LeCut to improve the performance of truncation and retrieval tasks simultaneously. ## **1 INTRODUCTION** 

Cut-off task aims to truncate ranking lists to balance the effectiveness and efficiency of retrieval results. This benefit is particularly significant in specific domains such as legal case retrieval, where the cost of assessing document relevance is high. Legal case retrieval aims to retrieve relevant legal cases from a corpus of candidate cases regarding a query case. In legal case retrieval, browsing all content in a ranking list takes much more effort than traditional IR tasks (e.g., Web search) mainly for two reasons: Firstly, users tend to examine more search results for legal case retrieval than Web search to find enough relevant information. As shown in Table 1, Shao et al. [25] observed that the number of visited pages per legal search session is over 4.6 times the number of Web search.

Also, the percentage of clicked pages to all pages in legal search is twice that of Web search. Secondly, users of legal search make decisions more slowly because of the long length and high reading complexity of legal documents. For example, the average document length of the legal dataset COLIEE [19]2021 is 4843 words, while for MSMARCO [17] it is only 1123. It can be concluded from Table 1 that users spend almost twice the time on a clicked page in legal search than Web search. In summary, the above evidence indicates that legal search involves a much higher search effort than Web search. Therefore, an effective truncation system that removes the irrelevant tail of the result list could potentially reduce legal search users’ mental or monetary cost. 

Previous truncation approaches [3, 13, 34] are mostly designed for traditional ad-hoc retrieval tasks and only take ranking scores and simple document statistics (e.g., document length and term frequency) as input. While such assumption-free methods can be applied to any type of cut-off task, they are limited by the ignorance of high-level document semantics in their modeling process. In legal case retrieval, the concept of relevance involves complicated semantic relationships between queries and documents. To capture this relationship requires more than ranking scores and document statistics. For example, Arampatzis et al. [1] proposed to find an optimal point to stop reading a ranking list in legal case retrieval based on the distribution of ranking scores. To make the model assumption-free, they designed a normal-exponential mixture to approximate score distributions under all scenes. However, such heuristic distribution functions cannot adapt to datasets with different score distributions, thus producing inferior performance in legal case retrieval. 

In this paper, we propose LeCut, a truncation model for legal case retrieval. To address the challenges above, LeCut utilizes hidden features in the retrieval task as additional information. Specifically, LeCut adopts a parallel framework that processes retrieval features to identify both document-wise and list-wise query-document similarities. Then, it uses a recurrent network followed by a Transformer layer with positional embeddings to capture the sequential dependencies in the ranking list. To the best of our knowledge, LeCut is the first to introduce semantic features and context information from the retrieval model to the cut-off task. 

In addition, existing efforts treat result list truncation as an isolated task instead of a component in the entire ranking process, which limits the usage of truncation in practical systems. Since the retrieval task and the cut-off task both focus on recognizing relevant documents, the information gather from each could benefit 

the other. Therefore, we propose a Joint Optimization of Truncation and Reranking (JOTR) framework, which jointly optimizes the retrieval model and the truncation model by taking their outputs as new features for each other. To adapt the information from LeCut to the legal retrieval model, we propose a mapping function to transform the original output of LeCut into proper cut-off features that could enhance the retrieval task. 

To better measure the effect of truncation models on improving the user experience in practice, we tailored and reconstructed an existing cut-off metric based on the user study of legal search. Experimental results on both Canadian and Chinese legal case retrieval datasets using widely-adopted and legal-specific cut-off evaluation metrics demonstrate the effectiveness of LeCut. Comparative experiments are conducted to validate the improvements made by JOTR. Further, we present a case study to visualize the improvement of cut-off predictions made by LeCut and how JOTR improves both tasks simultaneously. 

To summarize, our main contributions are: 

- We propose LeCut, a ranking list truncation model that novelly incorporates contextual information of the retrieval task into the prediction of optimal cut-off positions. 

- Based on LeCut, we propose a Joint Optimization of Truncation and Reranking (JOTR) framework to jointly optimize the retrieval and cut-off tasks. 

- Extensive experiments on different cut-off datasets and case studies demonstrate the effectiveness of LeCut and JOTR. 

The rest of our paper is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces the design and implementations of LeCut and JOTR. The adopted datasets, evaluation metrics, and experimental setup are described in Section 3. Then, Section 4 elaborates on the experimental results and analysis of our work on multiple datasets. Section 5 reviews the related work. Finally, Section 6 concludes our work and discusses directions for future work. The source code of our work is available at https://github.com/myx666/LeCut.

## **2.2 Feature Preparation** 

The first step of building a truncation model is to extract input features from query-document pairs. Existing studies [3, 13, 34] on ranking truncation mostly adopt relevance scores and document statistics for cut-off prediction. Although such a lightweight strategy is assumption-free and can be applied to any type of retrieval task, its ignorance of the contextual information in case documents could limit the effectiveness in legal case retrieval. To tackle this problem, we propose to extract three types of input features for LeCut: relevance scores, document statistics, and sentence-level embeddings in the retrieval task.

## **2.4 Joint Optimization of Truncation and Reranking** 

Previous researchers worked on the retrieval task and cut-off task separately. However, as the subsequent task of the retrieval task, the truncation of ranking lists also relies on the recognition of relevant documents. Thus, it’s potentially beneficial to tackle the cut-off and retrieval tasks with one optimization framework. In this paper, we propose JOTR, which works as a loop to jointly train the truncation and retrieval model. In the first iteration of JOTR, the retrieval model predicts a ranking list and corresponding relevance scores _𝑟𝑖_ for each document in the list. The ranking of documents at this stage depends on _𝑟𝑖_ entirely without other external information. Then, LeCut takes _𝑟𝑖_ and related features as the input of the first iteration to predict an initial list of cut-off confidence scores _𝑐_ = { _𝑐_ 1 _,𝑐_ 2 _, ...,𝑐𝑙_ }. After that, we rerank the previously retrieved results by introducing the cut-off information made by _𝑐_ to the retrieval model. 

While the idea of JOTR is straightforward, feeding the truncation model confidence score _𝑐_ to the retrieval model directly could hurt the retrieval performance. One reason for this phenomenon is the distribution disparity between _𝑐_ and the relevance score _𝑟_ . Ideal features for the retrieval model should have high correlations with the document relevance, but the original _𝑐𝑖_ does not follow this requirement as it is designed to measure the ranking performance of a truncated list with length _𝑖_ . Similar to Section 4.1, all experiments are conducted on six datasets. According to Table 5, the retrieval model with cut-off information exceeds its original performance across almost all the metrics on six datasets. Naturally, the optimized ranking list with better NDCG results will have a better cut-off performance using the same truncation model. JOTR is similar to the idea of multi-task learning [22], where both subtasks benefit from sharing the training data and part of the model parameters indirectly. Although the targets of retrieval and cut-off tasks are different, both tasks rely on predicting the score distribution of candidate cases. By transforming the cut-off confidence scores into relevance-related features through an appropriate formula, the iterative optimization process can be regarded as the approximation of two score distributions. 

## **4.3 Ablation Study** 

To investigate the effectiveness of different blocks used in LeCut, we conduct an ablation study on the ’LeCaRD + LFM’ dataset. The experiment does not discuss the utility of traditional document statistics and the attention mechanism already adopted and proved in previous works. As presented in Table 6, the truncation performance decreases without _𝑒𝑖_ or _𝛾𝑖_ feature. When both features are removed from LeCut at the same time, the performance decreases to a larger extent across three metrics. This suggests that _𝑒𝑖_ and _𝛾𝑖_ are both beneficial for predicting an ideal cut-off position. Although both features are generated by the same sentence-level embedding, they play a different role in LeCut. Specifically, the block generating _𝑒𝑖_ can be regarded as a dimensionality reduction approach to extract similarity between queries and candidates at the semantic level, while the other block generating _𝛾𝑖_ focuses on the deviation between the input embedding and the aggregated embeddings at previous positions in the ranking list. Therefore, the information mined by two blocks is supplementary to each other.

## **5 RELATED WORK** 

The idea of cutting off ranking lists to promote search efficiency was proposed in 1981 [29]. Identifying the optimal cut-off positions not only improves the non-monotonic metric such as F1 [3, 13, 34] but also serves as a prerequisite for retrieval systems with multiple stages [7, 16] because in such systems, the highly-effective model at

In addition, the cut-off task is also utilized to comprehend the prediction belief of retrieval models beyond the scope of a single relevance score [5]. In some professional domains requiring additional expertise, such as the legal or patent domain, the truncation of retrieval results significantly reduces users’ search effort [1, 6, 25]. 

As a specific Information Retrieval (IR) task, Legal case retrieval is essential to ensure legal justice. Relevant preceding cases can be crucial references or even directly involved in the decisionmaking process of a judgment [10]. Therefore, legal case retrieval has received growing attention in recent years [4, 28]. So far, there are mainly two categories of legal case retrieval models: Expert Knowledge-based models [23, 37] and Natural Language Processing (NLP) models. Recent effective models [9, 26, 27] are mainly based on NLP models. As a representative technique, models based on pretrained language models (PLMs) [2, 24, 32, 39] prevail on various legal tasks, including legal case retrieval. In this paper, we adopt hidden features from PLMs-based approaches for the cut-off task. 

## **6 CONCLUSION** 

This paper proposes LeCut, a ranking list truncation model which employs hidden features from the retrieval task to capture the semantic-level similarity between documents. In addition to training the truncation model in isolation, JOTR is proposed to optimize both the retrieval and cut-off task simultaneously. Experimental results on benchmark datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of LeCut and JOTR. A case study is conducted to visualize the cut-off positions of LeCut and how JOTR improves both tasks. 

On the other hand, our proposed work is generic and adaptive to other domains that require the efficiency of retrieval results. In the future, we will discover the application of LeCut and JOTR in other domains.
