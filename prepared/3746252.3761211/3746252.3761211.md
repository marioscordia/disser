## **Abstract** 

_Given a user query containing ambiguous and user-specific references, how can we effectively retrieve personalized information?_ Personalized information retrieval (PIR) requires resolving contextdependent cues — such as nicknames, personal locations, or temporal expressions. This poses challenges for conventional retrievers, including dense and generative models, which often struggle with entity ambiguity and generalization to user-specific contexts. 

In this paper, we propose PEARL ( **P** ersonalized **E** ntity- **A** ware Generative **R** etrieva **L** ), a novel generative retrieval framework for personalized IR. PEARL addresses key challenges through three components: (i) entity-aware annotation with span-level regularization to reduce lexical sensitivity, (ii) prefix-based contrastive learning to capture structural alignment between lexically divergent query-passage pairs, and (iii) context diversification to improve robustness against user-specific variations. 

Empirical results on both an existing PIR dataset and our new large-scale synthetic benchmark _PAIR_ show that PEARL consistently outperforms strong baselines under zero-shot evaluation. Notably, PEARL achieves the state-of-the-art performance in Hits@1 and MRR@10, demonstrating its effectiveness for retrieval in personalized user contexts. Our dataset is available at https://www. github.com/pearl-pair/pearl.

We adopt the generative retrieval paradigm for its ability to model structured, semantically disentangled representations of user intent. Personalized queries often involve context-specific entities — such as people, events, locations, and temporal expressions — with rich compositional structure. Generative retrieval accommodates this by producing hierarchical token sequences as passage identifiers, reflecting semantic paths (e.g., person → event → location) rather than mapping queries to a flat embedding space. 

This inductive bias enables finer disambiguation and robust generalization to user-specific references. In contrast to dense retrieval’s continuous similarity scoring, generative retrieval facilitates prefix-conditioned decoding, symbolic constraints, and interpretable alignment between query semantics and identifier structure. These properties are especially advantageous in PIR, where retrieval relevance depends not only on lexical matching but also on understanding implicit context and entity interactions. 

## **3 Proposed Method** 

In this section, we propose PEARL, a novel entity-aware generative retrieval framework for personalized information retrieval. Our approach addresses the following key challenges: 

- **C1 Lexical Disambiguation.** User-specific ambiguities arising from personalized contexts often lead to the retrieval of semantically irrelevant passages. _How can we retrieve the correct (gold) passage without relying on explicit lexical disambiguation of obscure terms grounded in personal context?_ 

- **C2 Discerning Semantic Structure.** Standard cross-entropy objectives struggle to distinguish between passages that are structurally similar but semantically distinct — particularly when similar entity types co-occur in comparable contexts. _How can_ 

   - _we train models to differentiate semantic intent despite structural overlap, capturing both similarity and semantic divergence?_ 

- **C3 Context-Invariant Representation.** Semantically equivalent queries or passages can appear in diverse surface forms due to paraphrastic variation in how meaning is framed or embedded in context, which may lead the model to infer divergent meanings. _How can we train the model to yield consistent representations for inputs that convey the same meaning despite syntactic and contextual variation?_ 

To address these challenges, PEARL unifies the following three core ideas into an end-to-end generative retrieval framework for accurate personalized information retrieval: 

- **I1 Semantic Expansion with Annotation (§3.1).** We enrich both queries and passages by annotating ambiguous or userspecific references with entity labels using a pre-trained NER model. These annotations enable the model to access higherlevel structural signals beyond surface-level semantics. To fully leverage this abstraction, we introduce entity-aware passage tokenization and entity-span mass regularization, which jointly guide the model toward learning more robust and disambiguated semantic representations. 

- **I2 Hard-Negative Prefix Contrastive Learning (§3.2).** We propose a contrastive training objective using hard negatives mined from a prefix-tree built via hierarchical semantic clustering. We also compute similarity scores with entity-structural embeddings that jointly encode high-level sentence structure and semantics, enabling the model to discern subtle semantic differences even among structurally similar candidates. 

- **I3 Context Diversification via Transposition (§3.3).** We introduce a data augmentation strategy that swaps entities between queries and passages with matching entity types and high semantic relevance. These transposed pairs retain core meaning but alter surrounding context, encouraging the model to learn context-invariant representations for semantically equivalent yet lexically diverse inputs.

## **3.1 Semantic Expansion with Annotation** 

Retrieval models frequently encounter difficulties when interpreting user-specific terms embedded in personalized contexts, often due to lexical ambiguity and insufficient structural cues. During preprocessing, we first identify ambiguous or user-specific mentions in both queries and passages using a pre-trained named entity recognition model _𝑔_ (·) [3]. The latter is an _entity-span mass regularization_ mechanism, designed to regulate attention allocation toward annotated entity spans, thereby promoting robust and generalizable semantic representations less reliant on ambiguous entities.

## **3.2 Hard-Negative Prefix Contrastive Learning** 

Standard cross-entropy training struggles to differentiate subtle semantic intent, particularly among passages exhibiting structural similarity or overlapping entity patterns. To address this, we integrate a structured contrastive learning objective that samples hard negatives from a semantically hierarchical prefix-tree built from entity-aware passage identifiers. Furthermore, we incorporate entity-structural embeddings into similarity calculations, ensuring the learned embeddings capture both structural information and subtle semantic differences. 

For instance, as shown in (I2) of Figure 1, given the query “When did I meet Mr. J at school?” and its positive passage “School Project Meeting at Mr. Johnson’s Office,” a suitable hard-negative would be “Orientation with Mr. Jackson at HQ Office.” Despite similar surface structure (e.g., person and location entities), the latter differs in contextual relevance, making it an effective contrastive example.

**Inference.** At inference time, we generate the most probable document identifier using autoregressive decoding with beam search. This highlights the unique advantage of our integrated framework, which jointly leverages entity-aware tokenization, contrastive learning, and context diversification, rather than relying on pipelinebased post hoc reranking. Overall, these results validate that PEARL not only achieves state-of-the-art performance but also provides a more robust and principled approach to personalized information retrieval in zero-shot settings.

## **5 Related Work** 

**Generative Retrieval Paradigms.** Generative retrieval formulates retrieval as a sequence-to-sequence task, allowing a unified model to perform both indexing and lookup. _Differentiable Search Index (DSI)_ [33], one of the earliest models, maps documents to tokenized identifiers (e.g., document IDs) and learns end-to-end retrieval using only transformer parameters. _Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI)_ [35] and _DSI-QG_ [40] further bridges the gap between indexing and retrieval by representing each document through multiple synthetic queries, aligning training closer to actual retrieval usage, while NCI incorporates prefix-tuned decoding to produce semantically meaningful identifiers. _MINDER_ [22] enriches semantic alignment using multi-view identifiers, assigning each document titles, n- grams, and pseudo-queries. Other works, such as [31], optimize ID tokenization to reduce generation ambiguity. Despite progress, generative retrievers remain content-agnostic and struggle with entity ambiguity — especially in personalized contexts, where the same mention may refer to different entities. We address this by incorporating Named-Entity Recognition (NER) during both indexing and retrieval.

**Entity Disambiguation in Retrieval.** Entity ambiguity is a long-standing challenge in information retrieval. The AmbER benchmark [5] shows that retrievers often exhibit popularity bias, favoring common entities when disambiguation cues are lacking. Traditional approaches have used NER and entity linking as preprocessing or auxiliary signals. Hasibi et al. [13] showed that annotating queries with linked entities improves ranking via feature augmentation. More recently, entity-aware sparse retrievers have emerged: Shehata et al. [30] enhance queries and documents with entity-linked expansions to boost recall, while De Cao et al. [9] use NER-derived context and type constraints to resolve ambiguity. However, most remain feature-based or pipelined. In contrast, our approach integrates NER into the generative retriever’s training loop, enabling context-aware indexing that combines the adaptability of generative models with reduced reliance on surface-form memorization. 

## **6 Conclusion** 

In this paper, we propose PEARL, a novel entity-aware generative retrieval framework for personalized information retrieval. To address ambiguous, user-specific references in queries, PEARL integrates three key techniques: entity-span mass regularization to reduce lexical sensitivity, prefix-based contrastive learning for structural alignment, and context diversification via transposition to improve robustness. These components are jointly trained under a unified sequence-to-sequence objective using semantically clustered identifiers derived from hierarchical _𝑘_ -means. Experiments on public and synthetic PIR datasets show that PEARL consistently outperforms sparse, dense, and generative baselines across all zero-shot retrieval metrics. Future work will explore continual user adaptation, scalable identifier construction, and cross-modal retrieval.

## **A GenAI Usage Disclosure** 

In this work, we synthetically generated the proposed dataset PAIR using large language models. Specifically, we used GPT-4o [1] from OpenAI via API calls for persona generation, and Qwen2.5-14BInstruct-1M [38] from Qwen for large-scale query-passage pair generation, running locally on 4× A100-80GB GPUs. 

The user profiles — personas — used to contextualize the querypassage pairs were generated via dedicated prompts designed to synthesize fictional users with realistic names, occupations, hobbies, frequent locations, and interpersonal relationships. 

Subsequently, query-passage pairs were created using three distinct prompt templates tailored to specific data types: (1) calendar events, (2) messages, and (3) contact entries. These prompts are conditioned on the generated user profiles, and include detailed instructions and examples to guide the models in producing realistic, contextually grounded, and diverse outputs. 

Examples of the prompts used during the generation process are provided in Sections A.1, A.2, A.3, and A.4. 

Followed by the generation process, all samples underwent a multi-stage, rule-based post-processing pipeline to ensure temporal consistency, contextual accuracy, and semantic diversity. Key steps include: 

**Date Consistency Adjustments.** Many queries include temporal expressions (e.g., "this weekend", "last month"), and the corresponding passage timestamps were programmatically adjusted to reflect these references accurately. For example, if a query referenced "this weekend" but the associated calendar event did not fall on the correct Saturday or Sunday, the passage was manually corrected. 

**Query Cleanup and Filtering.** Certain query patterns that were incoherent or semantically incompatible with their associated passages were either removed or rephrased. This includes queries such as "Who did I meet next weekend?" or "Did I go next month?". 

**Deduplication.** To reduce redundancy, samples were deduplicated in two phases. First, embedding-based deduplication was applied using TF-IDF and cosine similarity across queries and passage texts. Second, n-gram-based deduplication was performed by filtering out queries with overlapping tri-gram patterns after stopword removal and normalization.
