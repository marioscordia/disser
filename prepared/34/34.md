## **1. Introduction** 

Automated human action recognition has emerged as an important area within the field of computer vision, with many practical applications, including surveillance [1], security [2], human–computer interaction, fitness [3,4], and sports analysis [5]. In healthcare, the ability to recognize and understand actions, particularly in infants and young children, is especially valuable [6,7]. Similarly, accurate action recognition [8] plays a crucial role in ensuring children’s safety [9], monitoring their development, supporting early treatment for developmental delays, strengthening the parent–child bond, enhancing computer-based diagnostic tools, and expanding our understanding of child development [10]. 

However, one area that has not been adequately addressed is the detection of aggressive behaviors in children. Children’s mental development can be significantly impacted by aggressive behavior, which is 

often a response to frustration, stress, or an inability to self-regulate emotions, leading to different kinds of violations [11]. Such actions, if persistent, can disrupt social skills, hinder emotional regulation, and impair the ability to form healthy relationships. Children with ongoing aggressive behaviors often struggle in school, experiencing difficulties with concentration and learning, as well as conflicts with peers and authority figures [12]. Addressing these behaviors early is essential for promoting positive mental development, helping children learn to express their emotions in healthy ways, solve problems effectively, and build resilience. Support from parents, caregivers, and professionals is crucial in guiding children toward healthier behavior and emotional growth [13].

## **2. Related works** 

Recent advances in monitoring children’s activities have used various technologies and datasets to address safety and developmental challenges, such as tracking developmental milestones and recognizing unusual behavior patterns [28]. These technologies range from wearable devices to camera-based systems, each providing unique insights into children’s behavior in controlled and natural environments. In the following section, we review widely used datasets and methods in child activity recognition, highlighting their contributions and limitations in the context of aggressive activity recognition. 

## _2.1. Existing child activity datasets_ 

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in research and interest in child activity recognition due to its crucial role in various applications such as healthcare, safety, and understanding child behavior. Numerous video-based datasets have been developed, each addressing specific aspects of child activities, ranging from daily routines to medical monitoring and behavior analysis. 

These existing datasets cover a range of contexts and purposes. For instance, [29] proposed a dataset comprising 155 videos collected from open-source platforms like YouTube and Instagram, as well as custom videos. This dataset primarily focused on four key child activities: crawling, running, sleeping, and walking, which provide a foundational understanding of typical child behavior. In another notable effort, the authors of the study [30] collected videos specifically from three children with Down syndrome, labeling their activities such as crawling, sitting, standing, and walking. This approach adds a valuable perspective by focusing on children with specific developmental conditions. Other researchers have turned to more controlled environments for data collection. For instance, [31] utilized CCTV footage from daycare centers and supplemented it by labeling the AVA dataset [32]. This effort aimed to provide a robust training dataset for action recognition models in child-care settings. Similarly, Mohattola et al. [33] created an annotated dataset by selecting a subset from the Kinetics-600 dataset, categorizing videos based on whether the main performer was a child or an adult. This dataset focused on sports-related activities and helped distinguish child-specific actions from adult ones. 

There has also been an emphasis on identifying risky behaviors in children. For example, Ahmad et al. [34] developed a small but impactful dataset consisting of just four videos, with detailed annotations for frames that captured child activities within a home environment. This

Similarly, the global context-aware attention LSTM (GCA-LSTM) incorporates a recurrent attention mechanism that selectively emphasizes informative joints within each frame, enhancing the model’s performance in recognizing actions [40]. On the other hand, graph convolutional networks have emerged as powerful tools for modeling spatiotemporal skeleton data. The spatial–temporal graph convolution network (ST-GCN), for example, incorporates inter-frame edges to connect corresponding joints across consecutive frames, which significantly improves the understanding of temporal dynamics [41]. Additionally, 3D convolutional networks (CNNs) have been employed to capture spatial and temporal features from consecutive frames in skeleton data. For instance, PoseC3D utilizes heatmaps generated from body key points stacked along the temporal dimension, enabling 3D CNNs to effectively handle action detection [43]. Additionally, we have developed CABA_Net, specifically designed to recognize and classify these aggressive activities efficiently. 

## **3. Methodology** 

## _3.1. Child-aggressive behavior analysis dataset_ 

The proposed dataset of this study focuses on the recognition of child-aggressive behaviors as detailed in Table 2 within educational or home settings. The main goal is to understand these behaviors and reduce their impact on social environments. The activity classes represent specific types of aggressive behavior in children as presented in Fig. 2, which are carefully curated to reflect realistic actions of concern. The dataset provides an essential resource for developing models to detect aggressive behaviors in real-time, enabling early intervention and supporting a positive environment for children. 

## _3.2. Data collection_ 

Following data sourcing practices commonly adopted in large-scale video datasets such as Kinetics [44], ActivityNet [45], we collected videos from publicly available platforms, primarily YouTube.[1] Our focus was on content recorded in real-world settings such as homes, schools, and play areas to ensure the representation of unscripted, naturalistic child behavior. The dataset includes six predefined aggressive actions: “ _Hitting_ ,” “ _Throwing Object_ ,” “ _Kicking Surface_ ,” “ _Breaking Object_ ,” “ _Slamming Door_ ,” and “ _Pushing_ ”, all of which are commonly cited in behavioral and developmental literature. To promote global diversity and reduce cultural bias, we intentionally avoided the use of region- or culture-specific keywords when searching for videos. As a result, the dataset encompasses a variety of observable differences in environmental settings, clothing, and interaction styles, contributing to a more representative sample of the global population. Additionally, to simulate real-world deployment scenarios, we synthetically introduced variations in lighting conditions, such as low-light effects, using OpenCV. All videos were standardized to a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels to balance computational efficiency with spatial detail. 

## _3.3. Data annotation and pre-processing_ 

We meticulously reviewed each video, identifying and categorizing the different aggressive behaviors in children. Each video was cropped to isolate the relevant segments displaying the actions of interest, ensuring focused content for model training. Tools such as Canva[2] and Microsoft’s video editor[3] were used for precise cropping. To augment the dataset, we applied several data augmentation techniques using OpenCV. These included random rotation (±20[◦] ) and flipping to simulate different viewing angles, along with color and lighting adjustments to vary brightness, contrast, and saturation, mimicking natural changes in environmental lighting. Additionally, frame dropping was implemented by randomly omitting frames from video sequences to simulate imperfect video capture. These augmentations were applied to improve the model’s robustness and ensure its ability to generalize across different real-world scenarios. Moreover, to test the robustness of the proposed model under low-light scenarios, we applied the methods used in [46,47] to create a separate test set which includes low-light frames. This subset is used to evaluate the model’s performance under varied low-light situations.

## _3.6. Proposed model_ 

Our proposed CABA_Net is a hybrid deep learning model that integrates MobileViT for spatial feature extraction with Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) [48] and Attention-based LSTMs for temporal modeling. This architecture aims to handle both short-term and longterm dependencies in video-based activity recognition, making it particularly well-suited for detecting child-aggressive activities. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the video frames are passed from _𝑀𝑜𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑒𝑉𝑖𝑇_ to extract spatial features, generating a feature vector from a series of frames of the video. This feature vector is fed to the TCNs to capture short-term dependencies and attention LSTM modeling long-term dependencies to recognize activities in a real-world environment. 

_Novelty and Motivation:_ Unlike conventional models that rely solely on CNN-LSTM or transformer-based pipelines, CABA_Net introduces a novel integration of MobileViT, TCN, and Attention-based LSTM specifically designed for video-based child aggression recognition. The MobileViT module extracts rich spatial features by combining the local pattern extraction strength of CNNs with the global contextual awareness of transformers, all within a lightweight architecture suited for real-time and edge deployment. The TCN module captures short-term temporal dependencies using dilated convolutions, which is particularly effective for modeling quick, impulsive child behaviors. Finally, the Attention LSTM focuses on salient long-term dependencies by dynamically weighting key time steps, improving both performance and interpretability. To the best of our knowledge, this combination has not been explored in prior work and demonstrates a balanced design that enhances accuracy, temporal reasoning, and computational efficiency in the child aggression recognition domain. Each phase of the model is discussed in detail in the subsequent sections. The proposed model is formally described in algorithm 1, and the symbols and notations used are summarized in Table 4. 

## _3.7. Feature extraction with MobileViT_ 

The first component of CABA_Net involves extracting spatial features from individual video frames using pre-trained MobileViT [49]. The fundamental concept of MobileViT lies in merging CNNs with vision transformers (ViTs). This integration allows MobileViT to efficiently extract features from images while being suitable for deployment on mobile and edge devices. MobileViT captures the global context by combining convolutions and transformers. Convolutions focus on local spatial details, while transformers understand the relationships between different image patches to model long-range dependencies. These elements are fused, effectively combining local and global representations. As a result, MobileViT successfully bridges the strengths of CNNs in local feature extraction with the global context awareness of ViTs.

## **5. Limitations and future works** 

Although the newly introduced CABAD dataset and the proposed model lay a foundation for the research of child-aggressive behavior recognition using advanced artificial intelligence technologies, there are still several aspects of improvements that can be considered in future research. One significant limitation is the dataset’s reliance on videos sourced from public platforms like YouTube, which, while diverse, may lack the cultural and contextual richness required to generalize across different regions and social environments. The dataset also focuses on six predefined aggressive behaviors, which, although comprehensive for initial research, may not encompass all real-world scenarios of child aggression. Additionally, the evaluation primarily targets indoor environments, leaving the model’s robustness in outdoor settings unexplored. 

Another limitation lies in the reliance solely on visual data. Incorporating multimodal inputs such as audio cues, physiological signals, or contextual metadata could significantly enhance the model’s ability to detect nuanced behaviors and their triggers. The model also exhibit slight performance degradation in challenging conditions like lowlight scenarios despite its robustness. Addressing these issues requires further improvements in pre-processing techniques and architecture optimization to handle adverse environmental conditions better. 

For future work, expanding the dataset to include a broader range of cultural contexts, incorporating controlled, multi-cultural, and institutionally sourced video data, additional aggression scenarios, and outdoor environments would enhance its applicability. Developing models capable of integrating multimodal data can provide deeper insights into the emotional and situational triggers of aggressive behaviors. Furthermore, longitudinal studies that analyze temporal patterns of aggression over extended periods could provide valuable tools for early intervention and prevention strategies. These advancements would not only improve the generalizability of the framework but also contribute significantly to the fields of behavioral management and child welfare. 

## **6. Conclusion** 

This study introduced CABA_Net, a deep learning framework designed for automated child aggression recognition, trained on our newly developed CABAD dataset. Extensive evaluations confirmed its effectiveness in recognizing aggressive behaviors with improved accuracy, efficiency, and deployment feasibility compared to existing approaches. 

Beyond achieving state-of-the-art performance, CABA_Net shows practical benefits for real-world applications. The model balances high recognition accuracy with reduced computational overhead, making it suitable for real-time deployment in educational, home, and therapeutic environments. Additionally, its energy-efficient architecture ensures scalability for large-scale behavioral monitoring systems. Robustness evaluations further confirm its ability to maintain reliable performance under adverse conditions, including low-light environments, where CABA_Net achieves an average accuracy of 88% and F1-score of 87% across varying intensities. These results reinforce the model’s adaptability for deployment in diverse real-world scenarios, where lighting variations are common.
